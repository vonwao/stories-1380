# The Undreaming (opening)

The last time I called my sister, I asked to talk to her daughter, and my sister laughed and said, "Since when do I have a daughter?"

Her name was Valentina. She was six. She called me Tío Feo — Ugly Uncle — a joke she invented at four and enforced with total discipline for two years. I have a voicemail from March in which she sings me happy birthday in two languages, getting both wrong. I am the only person alive who knows any of this.

The first thing I felt was not grief. Grief came later and stayed. The first thing was embarrassment — the hot, specific embarrassment of being the crazy one on the phone, the relative people lower their voices about. My sister was laughing. My mother, when I tried her, was worse: she went quiet, and then she asked why I kept doing this to Ana, and her voice had the careful flatness you use on a man standing somewhere high.

I should say that the world had been off all year. Everyone agreed on that; nobody agreed on how. The turnpike exit near my apartment comes before its own sign now, and everyone just drives it that way, like the road is allowed to change its mind. The fourth floor of my building smells like a house fire that no inspection can find. There was a long argument online about whether February had been short even for February, thousands of people posting screenshots of a calendar that no longer said what they remembered it saying. People at work stopped telling each other their dreams, and then stopped having any to tell, and the word itself started to sound old-fashioned, like "telegram." The news called it ambient burnout. There was a hashtag.

I did the sane things. I checked her school's website. I searched my sister's accounts, which were full of her dogs and empty of her daughter, all the way back, seamlessly, six years of photographs recomposed with nobody missing from them, because you cannot be missing from a world that never contained you.

Then I did the insane thing, which was the only thing that worked: I played the voicemail. It is still there. Fourteen seconds. She rushes the second line the way she always did, and at the end she yells the word "feo" like a trumpet. I played it every night, on headphones, with the lights off, the way another man would drink. I could not have told you why it felt less like remembering and more like carrying — like something I was holding up out of water, and my arms were getting tired.

There is a playground two blocks from my building, on Dane Street, and I kept ending up at it without ever deciding to walk there. The fourth or fifth time, in the cold, a woman was sitting on the bench by the swings. Sixties, heavy, brown cardigan, eating sunflower seeds from her coat pocket with the unhurried rhythm of someone at a bus stop in no particular century.

"You can still hear her," she said. It was not a question, and she did not look up.

I stood there with my hands in my pockets and my heart going like I'd been caught shoplifting.

"Sit down," she said. "You've been on your feet for nine days."

Her name was Ruth. What she told me, on that bench, with the swings ticking in the wind, she told me in the tone of a woman explaining a bus schedule, and I am going to write it down the same way, because I have discovered that saying it carefully is the only way to say it at all.

Everything real is dreamed first, and kept dreamed. Places, weathers, people. Most of it keeps itself — eight billion sleepers is a lot of ballast. But some things are held up by one dream, or two. And when the dream that holds a thing goes down, the thing goes down with it, and the world closes over the place where it was like water. No hole. No wreckage. No funeral. You get a world where your niece is a story you know better than to tell at parties.

"There used to be three hundred of us on the night rolls," Ruth said. "There are eleven. Marisol keeps the Gulf. A man in Kraków keeps winter — real winter, the kind people your age think they're misremembering. I keep four city blocks and a language with nine speakers left." She folded the seed shells into her pocket. "And for about a year now, something has worked out that the fastest way to unmake a thing is not to attack the thing. It's to eat the dream that holds it."

I asked the question you're supposed to ask, which is: why me. Why could I remember what the whole world had agreed to forget?

"Because you're holding her," Ruth said. "Nine days now. Untrained, alone, out loud, on headphones. Nothing dreamed can be fully undreamed while one dreamer holds it — and nobody untrained has ever held anything for nine days." She looked at me for the first time, and her eyes were the tiredest and kindest thing I have ever been examined by. "That's what the exhaustion is. You thought it was grief. It's weight."

She stood, brushed her coat, and told me to go home and sleep, which no one had needed to tell me in a year.

"Tonight you'll find a red door," she said. "The others are waiting on the other side. Bring the song."

That night I slept nine hours for the first time since March, and the sleep had a floor to it, and a direction, like a stairwell. I found the red door exactly where a red door should never be, at the bottom of a stopped escalator, standing open.

Valentina was on the other side, waiting for me.

She was not alone.
