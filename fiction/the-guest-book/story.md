# The Guest Book

The rule at the guesthouse is one sentence.

Not a review. Not a prayer. Not an email address in case someone finds your scarf. One sentence in the book before you leave, written in whatever language you arrived with.

Anna learns this from Brother Peter, who is not a brother in the family sense or the friendly American sense, but an actual monk with actual keys on a ring large enough to defend himself with. The guesthouse sits below the monastery wall, three kilometers above the village, in a fold of beech forest where the road gives up twice before it gets there. Anna has been in Hungary six days and has already learned that roads can have opinions.

"One sentence," Brother Peter says. His English is careful and dry. "The monks do not read it."

"Then why write it?"

He gives her the look people give when the answer has been in the room longer than you have. "The next guest reads it."

The book lies open on a little table by the door. Black cover, cloth corners, pages swollen from years of damp hands. The left page is full of sentences in Hungarian, German, Polish, English, and one language Anna cannot place. The right page has one line, written by whoever slept in room three before her.

Lower the bucket before you sign.

That is all.

Anna reads it twice because the sentence is too ordinary to dismiss cleanly. Then she closes the book.

"Do people write jokes?" she asks.

"Often," Brother Peter says.

"Is this one?"

He does not look at the page. "That is not for us."

Anna is here because the hotel in Veszprem smelled like cigarettes and the lawyer moved the closing from Thursday to Friday and her grandmother's house has no heat in November. Her grandmother, Ilona Varga, died in March at ninety-one, in a hospital in Budapest, four counties away from the house where she was born. For seven months the house has sat in Kisborzsony with the shutters closed and the weeds coming up through the front path, waiting for the family to agree on a price.

Families can survive grief better than they survive property.

Her brother Tamas wants the sale finished because he has two children, one divorce, and a talent for finding expenses that arrive with a stamp. Anna wants it finished because she lives in Boston now, where property is something you rent from people who use phrases like natural light, and because a village house with a cracked roof is not an inheritance. It is weather with paperwork.

The buyer is a dentist from Gyor who wants the place for weekends. He has already sent drawings. Gravel drive. Summer kitchen removed. Well capped for safety. The old pear tree stays if possible, which in contracts means no.

Anna tells herself she is not sentimental. This is mostly true. She has three photographs of her grandmother and no recipes. Ilona did not give recipes. She gave corrections. More salt. Less water. You call that chopped? Anna remembers her sitting at the kitchen table in Kisborzsony with a blue apron over her dress, peeling apples into one long strip and refusing to say she was tired.

In the morning Brother Peter gives her coffee, bread, and plum jam in a jar with no label. The dining room is empty except for a German cyclist with calves like machinery, reading yesterday's weather from his phone as if it might apologize.

"You will sign today?" Brother Peter asks.

"If the dentist behaves."

This almost makes him smile.

At nine, Anna drives down through the forest to the village. The rented car is too wide for the lane, or the lane is too narrow for the century, one of those. Kisborzsony has one shop, one church, and a bus stop with a timetable that looks optimistic in both directions. The house is at the end of Fo utca, which means Main Street because villages are allowed to be funny without meaning to.

The key sticks. She has to shoulder the door the way her grandmother used to, hip first, as if entering a house were a small argument.

Inside, the rooms smell cold. Not bad. Just unaired. The stove tiles in the front room are green and cracked, and the table is still there, because no one in the family could agree whether it was worth shipping and no one in the village wanted another table with one short leg. The lawyer's folder is in Anna's bag. The closing is at two.

She walks through the house checking what she came to check. Window latches. Fuse box. Whether the buyer's man took the copper gutters, which he did not. She takes pictures for Tamas so he can feel involved from a distance.

The well is in the back, under a little roof gone gray with moss. Anna has seen it a hundred times. As a child she was told not to lean over it, not to drop stones in it, not to ask how deep it was because the answer changed depending on which adult wanted to frighten her. By the time she was twelve, the house had city water, and the well became a thing you warned children about. A hole with family history around it.

Lower the bucket before you sign.

She says no out loud, because the nice thing about an empty house is that it lets you be ridiculous without witnesses.

Then she lowers the bucket.

The rope complains. The pulley complains louder. The bucket drops and drops, knocking once against the stone, then lands with a sound Anna has not expected.

Water.

Not a dramatic amount. Not a biblical amount. A bucket amount. She hauls it up, hand over hand, and by the time it reaches the top her palms are wet and stinging. The bucket is half full, dark at first, then clearing in the light.

She is standing there with the rope in both hands when the neighbor comes through the gate carrying two yellow plastic jugs.

"Jo napot," the woman says, and then sees Anna's face and switches to the slow Hungarian people use for children and foreigners. "You are Ilona's granddaughter."

Anna says she is.

"Good. I thought maybe the dentist had already locked it."

The woman's name is Marika. She is in her seventies, wearing a black cardigan with holes at both elbows and rubber boots that have been repaired with tape. She takes the bucket from Anna without asking and fills her jugs with a plastic funnel she has brought in her pocket.

"The water in the village is off again," she says. "Only until noon, they say, so probably three."

"You use this well?"

Marika looks at her as if Anna has asked whether people use doors.

"When we need it."

"My grandmother let you?"

"Your grandmother charged me one jar of apricots every summer. Terrible price. I paid it."

Anna looks at the bucket, then at the capped-well clause sitting in her bag thirty meters away. Unused well to be filled and sealed by purchaser within ninety days of transfer.

"How many people?"

"For drinking? Three houses. Sometimes the church, when the pipe freezes. For gardens, more, but gardens are not people."

That sounds like something Ilona would say, and for one second Anna misses her with an anger that is almost physical. Not because of the well. Because her grandmother told everyone what to do for forty years and still failed to mention the one useful thing.

Marika caps the jugs. "You are selling."

"Yes."

"Good," Marika says, surprising her. "A roof needs money. Empty houses rot from the inside. People talk like a house has feelings because they do not want to discuss gutters."

Anna laughs once. It comes out harder than she means.

"But the well," Marika says.

"The buyer wants to cap it."

Marika lifts both jugs, one in each hand. She is small, but she does it easily. "Then the buyer should buy a different house."

At two o'clock Anna sits in the lawyer's office in Veszprem with the dentist, the dentist's wife, the lawyer, and a plate of biscuits nobody touches. The office has a printer that works in bursts, like it resents paper. The dentist is not a bad man. This is inconvenient. He wears a blue sweater and asks whether the drive from the village was all right. His wife has already chosen curtains on her phone.

Anna asks for an easement.

The lawyer pauses with his pen in the air.

"For what?"

"The well."

The dentist looks at his wife. The wife looks at the curtains, which have done nothing wrong.

Anna explains. Not beautifully. Not with a speech about community or memory or the old ways. She explains it the way Ilona would have: three houses use it when the village water fails; the church uses it when the pipe freezes; capping it would be stupid because a working well is not decoration. The lawyer says it will reduce the value. The dentist says there are liability issues. Tamas calls twice. She declines both calls and feels less like a person transporting a folder than a person making a decision.

They sign at four-thirty. The sale price is lower by eight thousand euros and the contract has two new paragraphs granting village access to the well path in water outages, which the lawyer writes as if he is punishing grammar itself. Tamas sends nine messages before dinner. Anna reads the first three and correctly understands the other six.

That night she drives back up to the guesthouse in the dark. The forest appears in the headlights one tree at a time. Brother Peter is in the entry hall replacing a bulb.

"You signed," he says.

"Yes."

"And the bucket?"

Anna stops taking off her coat.

He points to the guest book. "You read your sentence."

"The monks don't read it."

"No," he says. "But people come down from the hill with different faces."

In the morning, before she leaves, Anna stands over the book with a pen that has the monastery name on it and barely writes. The previous sentence has already been covered by hers in the order of the book; that is the small violence of every next thing.

She thinks about writing something grand and hates herself immediately. She thinks about writing, Do not trust dentists, which is unfair and not even funny enough. She thinks about her grandmother's apricot price. She thinks about Tamas, who will calm down once the transfer clears, and then become angry again in some new form because that is what brothers do when distance gives them room.

In the end she writes one sentence.

Ask who uses the thing you have been told is dead.

She closes the book. Outside, the German cyclist is oiling his chain in the cold, preparing to descend into weather he has definitely researched and cannot control. Brother Peter gives Anna her receipt in an envelope.

"Safe travel," he says.

On the road down, the village is still below the fog. The house is no longer hers. The well is no longer only the dentist's. The sentence behind her belongs to someone she will never meet.

She keeps both hands on the wheel all the way to the village.
