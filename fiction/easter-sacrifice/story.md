# The Easter Chocolate-Bunny Sacrifice

In Liebek, the Easter rabbit was not hidden.

It was carried through the front door on Good Friday under a white cloth, set on the family altar between two candles, and watched until Sunday morning. Then the oldest woman in the house cut off its head.

The rabbit was chocolate. This is important, although visitors still misunderstood the photographs.

Every house in Liebek had an altar for the Easter sacrifice. Some were permanent shelves with a cross, wedding pictures, and dead grandparents. Others were ironing boards brought out for the weekend and covered with a good tablecloth. The Martens family used a sewing table that had belonged to Marta's great-grandmother. One leg was shorter than the others. Each year her father corrected it with the same folded beer coaster, and each year her grandmother complained that the Son of God had risen without the help of a brewery.

The family rabbit was usually small. A person could hold it in one hand. The Martenses broke it after Mass, melted the pieces into hot milk, and drank from the blue cups that came out only for Easter and influenza.

The town rabbit was different.

It stood eighty centimeters tall and took three days to make. On Easter night it was sacrificed in the square in front of everyone. The confectioner chosen to make it received a brass plaque, free stall rent at the summer market, and one year of complaints from the other confectioners.

This year, the rabbit belonged to Marta.

"It belongs to Saint Agnes Chocolate," Marta corrected anyone who congratulated her. Saint Agnes was the shop. Marta owned it with her father, Emil, who owned forty-nine percent and behaved as if he owned sixteen.

"You made the recipe," Emil said.

"Oma made the recipe."

"Your grandmother dictated. You measured."

"You measured the first batch."

"That is how we learned you should measure."

He said this without embarrassment. Emil considered mistakes to be work he had delegated to the future.

Saint Agnes Chocolate occupied two narrow rooms across from the old post office. The front room held the finished sweets. The back room held molds, copper bowls, a tempering machine older than Marta, and a freezer that opened if kicked near the lower hinge. Emil made truffles and avoided paperwork. Marta did everything else.

The shop had been losing money for three winters.

Liebek had four thousand people, three churches, five confectioners, and a supermarket that sold chocolate rabbits for less than Saint Agnes paid for cocoa. Tourists came in summer for the crooked bell tower and the houses along the canal. At Easter they usually went somewhere larger.

Then a photograph of the sacrifice appeared online. It showed the mayor in black, Marta's grandmother holding the ceremonial knife, and a chocolate rabbit beneath fourteen candles. The caption said:

> CHRISTIAN CULT IN EASTERN EUROPE EXECUTES EASTER BUNNY

The photograph was seven years old. The knife was a cake server. The mayor was in black because the town choir required it. None of that slowed the photograph down.

By Thursday, two television crews had arrived in Liebek. The mayor ordered new bunting. The tourism office printed six hundred leaflets called THE SWEET MYSTERY OF LIEBEK. A company that made protein bars offered to sponsor the sacrifice if the rabbit wore a small edible sash.

Marta said yes before she had seen the sash.

Her grandmother said no after seeing only the envelope it came in.

"A sacrifice does not have a sponsor," Oma Hanne said.

They were in the back room of the shop. Marta had the rabbit mold laid open across the steel table. Each half was the size of a child.

"The church has sponsors," Marta said.

"The roof has donors."

"Their names are on plaques."

"The roof is not killed in public."

Emil was pouring almonds into the grinder. He looked from his daughter to his mother and decided the almonds required his full attention.

The sponsor sash said VIGORSPRING in white letters on red. It tasted like vanilla and floor polish.

"They are paying eight thousand," Marta said. "Do you know what eight thousand is?"

"A lot to wear something ugly."

"It is the electricity through September."

Oma Hanne touched the inside of the mold with one finger. At eighty-two, she had small square hands and the quick eyes of somebody who had never believed a child with crumbs on his shirt. She lived above the shop, refused to use the lift in the new medical building, and cheated at skat with such consistency that it had become one of the rules.

"The rabbit must be plain," she said.

"The rabbit is already eighty centimeters tall and carried through town behind an accordion. Plain has left the room."

Emil stopped the grinder.

"The accordion is not required."

Both women looked at him.

"I only mean historically," he said, and started the grinder again.

They compromised on a smaller sash.

The rabbit took shape Friday morning.

Marta painted the mold by hand with dark chocolate, let the shell set, then added three more layers. Emil watched the temperature. Oma Hanne sat on a stool shelling hazelnuts into two bowls, good and bad, moving some back and forth according to standards nobody else could see.

Klara sat under the steel table and asked questions.

Klara was Marta's nine-year-old niece. Her mother had gone to Warsaw for work and would be back Sunday morning if the train did what it had promised, so Klara had been installed above the shop with Oma Hanne for the week. She had lost one front tooth and carried a green backpack full of objects she considered possibly important. A spoon. String. Two batteries. A photograph of a horse she did not know.

"Does it hurt?" Klara asked.

"What?"

"When you cut it."

"The rabbit is chocolate."

"That wasn't my question."

Marta leaned down. "No. It does not hurt."

"How do you know?"

"Because we make it."

Klara considered this. "God made us."

Oma Hanne dropped a hazelnut into the bad bowl.

"She has you there," Emil said.

"You are not helping."

He checked the thermometer again, although the number had not changed.

Klara had been worried about the rabbit since Monday. Marta found three carrot peels beside the mold that evening and a bottle cap filled with water. She threw them away before Oma Hanne saw.

On Good Friday afternoon, they joined the two halves. Emil warmed the edges on a flat pan while Marta held them upright. Before sealing the base, Oma Hanne placed three things inside: a pinch of salt, one hazelnut, and a small paper cross.

Nobody in the family agreed why.

Oma Hanne said the salt was for the tears at the cross, the hazelnut for new life, and the paper cross because otherwise it would be only dessert.

Emil said his grandmother had done it because chocolate used to be expensive and hollow rabbits disappointed children.

Marta suspected both stories had been invented after the fact.

"What if somebody swallows the cross?" Klara asked.

"It is paper," Oma Hanne said.

"That doesn't answer it."

"Then they will be especially blessed."

The completed rabbit stood on the worktable with its ears almost reaching the hanging pans. It had dark-chocolate eyes and a red ribbon at its neck. The small VIGORSPRING sash crossed its chest.

Marta had expected to feel proud.

Instead she noticed that one ear leaned half a centimeter to the left.

"Don't touch it," Emil said.

"I'm looking."

"You look with your fingers."

She moved her hands behind her back.

The television crew arrived while the chocolate was still setting. A young reporter named Daan wanted footage of the family "preparing the altar of sacrifice." Marta explained that the town altar would be built Saturday.

"Could you prepare something now?"

"We are preparing the rabbit."

"I mean candles."

"The chocolate does not need candles."

Daan looked past her toward Oma Hanne, who had changed into a black dress without telling anyone and was now sorting hazelnuts beside the rabbit.

"Could she hold the knife?"

"No."

"The cake server," he said.

"Still no."

Oma Hanne held out her hand. "Give it."

The camera light came on. Daan asked her what the sacrifice meant.

"It means Easter," she said.

"And the rabbit represents?"

"Chocolate."

"Is there a deeper significance?"

"If you eat slowly."

Marta heard Emil laugh in the grinder room.

The clip was online by dinner.

Saturday morning the line outside Saint Agnes reached the post office. People bought every small rabbit in the shop, then bought marzipan lambs they did not seem to want. Daan's cameraman bought two bags of hazelnut bark. The sponsor called to ask whether Oma Hanne could mention "renewal through vitality" during the ceremony.

Marta said no.

The sponsor offered another thousand.

Marta said she would ask.

She did not ask.

At four, they closed the shop and prepared to carry the rabbit to the square.

It was gone.

The white cloth lay on the steel table. The stand was empty. One of the back windows was open six centimeters.

Marta looked under the table, which was ridiculous. Emil checked the freezer. Oma Hanne went upstairs without a word.

"It weighs eleven kilos," Marta said. "Nobody carried it out the window."

"Maybe they took it in pieces," Emil said.

"Why would it be in the freezer?"

"People put things places."

The brass band had started tuning in the square. Through the front glass they could see the mayor crossing the street in his black coat. The sponsor's representative was with him, wearing a red scarf the exact color of the sash.

Marta went upstairs.

Oma Hanne stood in Klara's room looking at the green backpack on the bed. It contained the spoon, the string, two batteries, the photograph of the horse, and a small hacksaw.

"Was that in there before?" Marta asked.

"No."

They found the first chocolate mark on the back stairs. A thumbprint, low on the wall. Then another on the door to the alley.

The trail continued across the courtyard, into the old washhouse, and stopped.

Klara sat behind the boiler with the town rabbit laid on a folded blanket. She had removed the sponsor sash. The rabbit's left ear was missing.

"What happened to the ear?" Marta asked.

Klara covered her mouth.

Oma Hanne closed the washhouse door behind them.

"I had to know," Klara said through chocolate.

"Know what?"

"If he was solid."

"You could have asked."

"You said hollow."

"Then why did you saw off his ear?"

"I didn't believe you."

This was so entirely a family answer that nobody spoke for a moment.

The rabbit had suffered during transport. Its base was cracked. One hind leg had collapsed inward. The right hand held a carrot; the left had lost three fingers.

From the square came the first bars of the procession hymn, followed by an amplified squeal.

"We have forty minutes," Marta said.

"No," Oma Hanne said.

"I can repair it."

"It will split when you lift it."

"Then we carry the stand."

"And when the mayor strikes it?"

"He doesn't strike it. You cut it."

Oma Hanne sat on an upturned bucket. She looked at the rabbit, then at Klara.

"Why did you take him?"

"Because everybody was happy about killing him."

"We are going to eat him."

"That's worse."

Emil came in, saw the damage, and sat beside his mother on another bucket.

"We have the supermarket rabbit," he said.

Marta stared at him.

"For the window," he said. "The big gold one."

The supermarket had donated it after the photograph went viral, along with a printed sign saying COMMUNITY PARTNERS. It stood almost as tall as theirs and weighed half as much. Marta had put it in the storage room because its face was cheerful in an insulting way.

"We cannot sacrifice that," Oma Hanne said.

"It is chocolate," Emil said.

Oma Hanne pinched a piece from the broken base of their rabbit, tasted it, and looked at her son.

"The other one is not."

It was legally chocolate. Marta checked the label. Cocoa-flavored compound coating, sugar, vegetable fat, whey powder. The shell was reinforced inside by a clear plastic frame.

"The cameras won't taste it," Emil said.

Oma Hanne looked at him.

"I am only stating the cameras' limitations."

They carried the supermarket rabbit to the square.

It went under the white cloth. The sponsor's representative attached a full-size VIGORSPRING sash before Marta could stop him. The mayor thanked the visiting media, the tourism office, and "the Martens confectionery family, guardians of this beautiful and somewhat misunderstood tradition."

Oma Hanne stood beside the altar with the silver cake server.

Marta could tell by the way she held it that they were in trouble.

The crowd counted down from three. This was new. Marta hated it.

Oma Hanne brought the server down across the rabbit's neck.

It bounced.

The head wobbled, smiling.

She struck again. The server chipped the coating and hit the plastic frame with a clean knock that the microphone carried over the square.

Children laughed. Then adults did. The mayor bent close and whispered something.

Oma Hanne handed him the cake server.

"You sponsored it," she said into the microphone. "You finish."

The sponsor's representative disappeared into the crowd.

The mayor tried sawing. The brass band, uncertain, played two quiet measures of the hymn and stopped. Daan's cameraman moved closer.

Marta saw the next morning's photograph before it existed. CHRISTIAN CULT UNABLE TO EXECUTE EASTER BUNNY.

Then Klara came through the crowd carrying one half of the real rabbit.

Emil followed with the other half on the stand. The crack had opened during the walk. One leg was gone. The remaining ear leaned left.

Klara had tied the small sponsor sash around her own coat.

"Use this one," she said.

The square settled.

Oma Hanne came down from the platform. She stood in front of Klara and the broken rabbit.

"You agree now?"

Klara looked at the missing ear in her hand. "Only if I do it."

The mayor started to object. Oma Hanne gave him the look that had sorted hazelnuts for seventy years, and he stopped.

They put the rabbit on the altar. Up close, the damage was worse. The shell had softened in the washhouse. Chocolate marked Emil's sleeves and Klara's hair. It looked handled. It looked like food from a family kitchen instead of an object from a window.

Oma Hanne put her hand around Klara's on the server.

"Not the neck," Klara said.

"Where?"

Klara pointed to the middle.

Together they pressed the blade into the rabbit's chest.

The shell gave.

Dark-red liquid ran out across the white cloth.

There was a sound from the crowd. Not a scream. Six hundred people taking the same breath.

Marta smelled cherries.

Emil looked at her.

"You filled it?"

"No."

Oma Hanne dipped one finger into the liquid and tasted it. Her eyes moved to Klara.

Klara shook her head so hard the sponsor sash slipped from her shoulder.

The cherry cordial ran around the candles and dripped from the altar to the stones. Daan's camera was close enough to catch every drop. Father Pawel, who had attended in civilian clothes so he could deny church involvement, crossed himself.

"Well," Oma Hanne said. "It has happened before."

"When?" Marta asked.

"Not to me."

That was all she would say.

The rabbit broke easily after that. People came forward with cups and pieces of bread. The mayor received a section of the undamaged hindquarters. Father Pawel took one ear and tried to hide it in a napkin. Klara ate nothing. She stood by the altar and watched until the last pieces were gone.

The supermarket rabbit remained on the other table, smiling through its wound.

By Monday, Saint Agnes had orders from twelve countries. The sponsor requested rights to the phrase RENEWAL THROUGH VITALITY. Marta declined. The tourism office asked whether the bleeding could be repeated hourly during the summer.

"No," she said.

"Could you do one demonstration?"

"No."

Emil took the call next and said maybe.

Oma Hanne put the brass plaque in a drawer because she did not like the font. Klara returned the hacksaw to the caretaker at school without explaining why she had borrowed it.

For the next month, tourists came into the shop asking for the Blood Rabbit. Marta made ordinary rabbits. Some leaned left. Some had thin spots. None of them bled.

The broken plastic supermarket rabbit stood in the window with a handwritten card:

> COMMUNITY PARTNER. NOT FOR EATING.

Near Pentecost, while cleaning behind the flour tins, Klara found the missing chocolate fingers from the real rabbit. They were lined up neatly on the floor, smallest to largest.

Beside them was the little paper cross.

Klara called Marta into the room.

"Did you take that out?" Marta asked.

Klara shook her head.

The hazelnut was gone.

They looked beneath the shelves. There was no mouse, no hole, and no chocolate rabbit. Only a thin red mark running behind the wall, still wet enough to shine.

From upstairs came Oma Hanne's voice.

"If you two are finished crawling around, the milk is getting cold."

Klara picked up the paper cross.

"Should we tell her?"

Marta looked at the red line. It disappeared where the floor met the wall.

"After breakfast," she said.

They went upstairs. The blue cups were already on the table.
