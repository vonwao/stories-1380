# Unit 214 Has No Door

Nestor Velez knew every door in the Seabreeze Professional Center because counting them was part of his job.

There were twenty-six tenant doors, six service doors, three stairwell doors, two doors in the lobby restrooms, and a narrow metal door behind the dumpster that no key on the property ring could open. There was no Unit 214.

The directory went from 212, Ocean Mediation, to 216, Feet First Podiatry. Between them hung a framed photograph of a sailboat.

At 2:14 on Thursday morning, Nestor saw light under a door between the two offices.

He was looking at Camera Seven. Camera Seven showed the second-floor east corridor, where nothing moved after the mediation office locked up at eight. On the monitor, the photograph of the sailboat was gone. In its place stood a narrow door with frosted glass. Gold light shone beneath it.

Nestor looked through the security window into the real lobby. Empty.

He looked back at the monitor.

The door was still there.

He switched Camera Seven off and on. The door remained. He switched to Camera Six and saw the empty stairwell. Camera Eight showed the dark west corridor and an artificial ficus somebody had abandoned outside the divorce accountant.

He called Denise.

She answered on the seventh ring. "If this is about the yogurt, I dated it."

"What yogurt?"

"Then never mind."

"Where are you?"

"Third floor."

"Come down to the desk."

"No."

"Please."

"You found a body?"

"No."

"Water?"

"No."

"Fire?"

"No."

"Then it can wait until I finish the dentist."

Nestor watched the door.

Something passed behind the frosted glass. Not a person exactly. A dark upright shape, followed by another, smaller one. Then the handle turned.

"Denise."

"I'm coming."

Denise Ward had cleaned Seabreeze for twelve years and considered the words facilities management a personal insult. She arrived pushing a gray cart with a yellow trash bag tied to one side and four dying office plants on the other.

"Camera Seven," Nestor said.

She looked at the monitor. Her face changed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You know that door?"

"No."

"You made a face."

"I make faces all night. It's how I stay awake."

On the monitor, the handle stopped moving.

Denise leaned closer. She smelled of lemon disinfectant and the cinnamon gum she chewed to keep from smoking. "What time is it?"

"Two sixteen."

"When did it come on?"

"Two fourteen."

"Of course."

"Of course what?"

She took a ring of keys from her apron and pushed the cart behind the desk.

"Where are we going?"

"Second floor."

"There's no door there."

"Then this should be quick."

The second-floor corridor was dark except for the emergency lights. Ocean Mediation had a ceramic bowl outside full of white stones with words painted on them: BREATHE, LISTEN, RELEASE. Somebody had stolen TRUST the week before.

Nestor stopped at the sailboat photograph.

No door. No gold light. The wall felt cool under his hand.

Denise did not touch it. She stood several feet back and looked first at Ocean Mediation, then Feet First, then the ceiling.

"They lowered it," she said.

"Lowered what?"

"The ceiling."

"It's an office building. They always lower ceilings."

"Not when I started here."

She walked to the east stairwell. Above the door was a box-shaped emergency light with two plastic lamps. One pointed down the stairs. The other pointed at the wall.

The second lamp was on.

It was an old yellow bulb, warmer than the white emergency lights. Its beam crossed the corridor and landed on the sailboat. Under the glass, behind the photograph, faint raised letters showed through the paint.

214.

Nestor lifted the frame from its hook. The wall behind it was covered in pale rectangles where old signs had been painted over. He could make out a word in blue.

LIGHT.

"Wishing Light," Denise said.

"What is that?"

"A store."

"Here?"

"Before here."

The warm bulb went out.

The letters disappeared. The wall became a wall.

Back downstairs, the door had vanished from Camera Seven.

Nestor opened the incident log.

Denise put one hand over the keyboard.

"Don't."

"A light turned on."

"Lights do that."

"A door appeared on camera."

"Doors don't."

"That's why I'm reporting it."

"Mr. Solt reads those."

"Mr. Solt pays me to write them."

"Mr. Solt pays you to write what he can send to an insurance company."

Nestor moved her hand.

"Fine," she said. "Put down that the ghost store came back. Put my name under Witness. Spell it right when they fire us."

He wrote:

> 02:14. Visual anomaly on Camera 7. Old emergency lamp activated outside Suites 212/216. No sign of forced entry, fire, or electrical damage. Camera functioning normally after 02:19.

"Coward," Denise said.

"Employed coward."

She untied the yellow bag from her cart. "For now."

At seven, Mr. Solt arrived carrying a cardboard tray with three coffees, one for himself and two for the men following him. The men wore hard hats and bright vests, although they were indoors.

"Nestor. Good. These are the survey guys."

Mr. Solt managed the building for a company in Boca that owned fourteen properties and liked to name each one after the thing it had replaced. Seabreeze had no view of the sea. Its east windows faced a tire warehouse.

"Survey for what?"

"Ownership is exploring options."

"Selling?"

"Exploring."

The older surveyor unfolded a plan across the security desk. Mr. Solt moved Nestor's coffee out of the way.

"We're doing wall measurements tonight," Mr. Solt said. "Some test openings. Minimal noise."

"Tonight?"

"Starting at two. You can give access."

Nestor looked at the plan. It showed the second floor as a clean rectangle. Suites 212 and 216 shared a wall six feet thick.

"Why is that wall so wide?"

The older surveyor leaned over. "Old stair, maybe. Dumbwaiter chase. This place is layers."

"We have a stair."

"Probably another one before the remodel."

Mr. Solt tapped the incident book. "You see a ghost?"

"A camera anomaly."

"Good correction."

"And an emergency lamp came on."

"That building is sixty years of emergency lamps. Denise probably hit a switch."

"I did not," Denise said from behind him.

Mr. Solt turned. She was holding a small fern in a coffee cup.

"How long have you been there?"

"Long enough."

"Plants can't stay at the desk."

"This one has seventy-two hours."

"Until what?"

"I decide."

She put it beside Nestor's monitor.

Mr. Solt gathered the plans. "Tonight, no wandering tenants, no cleaning cart in the east corridor, and no anomalies unless they require the fire department."

After he left, Denise gave Nestor the fern.

"You said it was dying."

"It is."

"Why give it to me?"

"You like procedures."

Nestor's weekend began at eight. He drove home, slept until noon, and picked up his daughter from her mother's apartment at four.

Belén was nine and had recently decided that adult conversation was something she could survive by making slow circles with her head. She made one while Nestor explained that the marine center would have to be Sunday instead of Saturday.

"Work," he said.

"I heard."

"They're making changes at the building."

"You said we were going."

"We're going Sunday."

"The octopus is fed at eleven Saturday."

"There's still an octopus Sunday."

"Not the same octopus."

It was the same octopus. He did not say so.

At his apartment she watered the desk fern until water ran through the coffee cup and onto the incident-report study guide he had brought home.

"Denise says seventy-two hours."

"Denise says a lot of things."

"Is she your boss?"

"No."

"You do what she says."

"I listen."

"Mom says that's how they get you."

Belén wandered off to inspect the freezer.

Nestor was studying for the state electrical maintenance exam, which he had failed once by three points. Mr. Solt had told him a license might turn his security job into an assistant facility job. It would mean mornings, a little more money, and Saturdays with the correct octopus.

He opened the guide to emergency lighting.

The old bulb outside Unit 214 had no wire running to it.

He found that out at one forty-five Sunday morning, before the surveyors arrived. He removed the faceplate and traced the empty metal conduit up into the ceiling. It ended in plaster.

Denise watched from her cart.

"When were you going to tell me about Wishing Light?"

"I did tell you. It was a store."

"What kind?"

"Repair."

"Lamps?"

"Anything small enough to bring in. Lamps, radios, irons, clocks."

"Why wishing?"

"The owner's name was Wysing. W-Y-S-I-N-G. The sign painter spelled it the other way, and Mr. Wysing was too cheap to repaint."

"You saw it?"

"My mother did."

"Here?"

"Here before here. There was a row of stores with rooms above. Wishing Light, a bakery, shoe repair, two places that changed every year. They knocked most of it down and put this box over what was left."

"Your mother lived upstairs?"

"For a little while."

"Why didn't you say any of this?"

"Because you make a report when yogurt moves."

"Yogurt doesn't move."

"Mine did."

The surveyors arrived at two with drills, plastic sheeting, and a portable lamp so bright it flattened every shadow in the corridor. Mr. Solt came with them. He wore jeans and an old polo shirt and looked younger without his office clothes.

They taped plastic from Ocean Mediation to Feet First.

"We'll start with the center," the older surveyor said. "There should be a void."

"Wait until two fourteen," Denise said.

Mr. Solt looked at Nestor.

"She's joking," Nestor said.

"I am not."

At two twelve, the surveyor marked the wall with blue chalk. At two thirteen, he lifted the drill.

The old emergency bulb came on.

Its gold beam was weak against the work lamp. Still, the chalk mark disappeared. Raised letters showed through the paint.

WISHING LIGHT

The younger surveyor took off his safety glasses.

"Was that there?"

"Paint layers," the older man said. "Angle of illumination."

Something knocked from inside the wall.

Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

Denise dropped her roll of trash bags.

"Mom?"

Mr. Solt turned off the work lamp.

The narrow door stood between 212 and 216.

This time they could all see it without the camera. Frosted glass. Brass handle. Gold light underneath.

On the glass, in small blue letters:

REPAIRS WHILE YOU WAIT

Mr. Solt said, "Nobody touch anything."

Denise opened the door.

On the other side was a shop no wider than a hallway. Lamps hung from the ceiling, stood on shelves, crowded the floor. Table lamps with cloth shades, green banker's lamps, bare bulbs, a red glass lantern with a crack down one side. Every one was lit. Their cords went nowhere.

A counter ran along the back. Behind it sat a man in a white shirt and dark vest, asleep with his cheek on a repair ticket.

The air smelled of hot dust.

Denise went in. Nestor followed her. The younger surveyor followed Nestor. Mr. Solt told them all to come back and then entered too.

The older surveyor stayed in the corridor.

"I'm not insured for this," he said.

The sleeping man lifted his head.

"Ticket," he said.

He had a narrow gray face and a pencil behind each ear.

"I'm Denise Ward."

"Ticket."

"My mother was Odessa Ward."

"Ticket."

"She lived upstairs."

The man opened a wooden drawer with hundreds of paper stubs standing on edge. "Everybody lived upstairs."

Denise took a breath that shook.

"She left something here."

"Everybody left something here."

"A blue overnight case."

The man looked at her properly.

"Date?"

"Nineteen seventy-six. Maybe seventy-seven. She said the hurricane shutters were up. She had to leave quickly."

"They all had to leave quickly."

He began moving through the paper tickets. His fingers were stained black at the tips.

Nestor looked behind the counter. The back wall was not a wall. Beyond it, a narrow street ran under a dark sky. A bakery window shone across the way. A bicycle leaned against a post. People moved in the distance carrying suitcases and boxes.

"What's out there?" he asked.

"Closed," the repairman said.

"It doesn't look closed."

"That's how it gets you."

Mr. Solt was examining the floor with his phone flashlight. "This room is not on the structural plan."

"Young man," the repairman said, "neither are you."

The younger surveyor laughed once and covered it with a cough.

The repairman pulled out a ticket.

"Odessa Ward. Blue case. One table fan, one photograph, child's yellow dress, incomplete."

"Incomplete what?"

"Repair."

"Can I have it?"

"Ticket."

"She lost it."

"Then she should come herself."

"She died in 2008."

He looked toward the street behind him.

"That is inconvenient."

From the corridor came the sound of the drill starting.

Mr. Solt turned. "I told him to wait."

The shop lights trembled. A lamp fell from a shelf and smashed. The street beyond the counter went dark one window at a time.

The repairman stood.

"Closing."

"You just opened," Nestor said.

"Same thing from this side."

Denise reached across the counter and seized the ticket.

"Ma'am."

"She told me the case was full of money."

"It says no money."

"She told everybody a different story. I want to see which one she told herself."

The wall shook. Plaster dust fell from the ceiling.

Mr. Solt ran into the corridor. "Kill the drill!"

The younger surveyor backed toward the door. Nestor stayed with Denise.

The repairman found a blue case beneath the counter. It was small, with a broken handle held together by white tape.

"Incomplete," he said again.

Denise took it.

The door behind them began to close.

Nestor grabbed the handle. It pulled hard enough to lift his shoes from the floor.

"Come on."

Denise held the case against her chest. The repairman was already turning away, carrying the broken lamp toward a workbench.

"What happens to you?" Nestor called.

"I close."

"And the street?"

"It was closed before you came."

The door narrowed. Nestor saw the bakery window, the bicycle, a woman hurrying across the street with a stack of folded sheets. The repairman put on wire-framed glasses and reached for a screwdriver.

Denise stepped through.

Nestor followed. The brass handle came out in his hand.

The door vanished.

They stood in the corridor with Mr. Solt and the two surveyors. The drill lay on the carpet. In the wall was a round hole six inches deep, ending in concrete.

Denise sat against Ocean Mediation's door and opened the blue case.

Inside were a child's yellow dress, a black-and-white photograph, and a table fan wrapped in newspaper. No money.

The photograph showed two women behind the counter at Wishing Light. One was young Denise, maybe ten, wearing the yellow dress. The other was her mother.

Between them stood the gray-faced repairman, exactly as he had looked five minutes before.

"That's impossible," Mr. Solt said.

Denise turned the photograph over.

On the back, her mother had written:

> Denise refuses to smile because Mr. Wysing would not let her touch the radios.

Denise laughed. It came out mixed with something else.

Nestor sat beside her.

"What did she tell herself?"

"That we'd come back in the morning."

Mr. Solt lowered himself onto one of Ocean Mediation's white stones. RELEASE turned beneath his shoe.

"Ownership wants this wall opened by Monday."

"You saw what happened," the younger surveyor said.

"I saw an unpermitted room."

"You spoke to a dead man."

"I don't know that he was dead."

"He was in a wall."

"This is Florida."

No one had a response to that.

Mr. Solt picked up the brass handle. "Can this be installed somewhere else?"

Nestor looked at him.

"What? I'm asking."

They did not open the wall that night.

By Tuesday, ownership had decided the concrete behind Units 212 and 216 was a structural support and should not be disturbed until an engineer came. Mr. Solt changed the survey report himself. He did not mention the shop.

Denise took the photograph home. She left the fan with Nestor because the cord had been cut and he was studying electrical repair.

"Mr. Wysing couldn't finish it," she said. "Maybe you can."

Nestor replaced the cord and cleaned forty years of black dust from the motor. The fan ran for six seconds, stopped, and ran backward. The second time he tried, it blew warm air that smelled like bread.

He unplugged it.

On Saturday, he took Belén to the marine center. They missed the octopus feeding by eight minutes.

"You said eleven."

"My clock was wrong."

"Your whole thing is clocks."

"That's watches."

"Same family."

She pressed both hands to the aquarium glass. The octopus was hiding in a clay pot with one eye showing.

"Denise gave us a fan," he said.

"Does it work?"

"Not correctly."

"Good."

On Thursday night, Nestor brought the fan to the security desk. The fern beside Camera Seven had grown one new leaf.

At 2:14, the fan turned on by itself.

Warm air moved across his hands. Camera Seven flickered.

For a moment the corridor showed the usual sailboat between 212 and 216. Then the picture shifted. The narrow door was back, its glass dark. Across it hung a paper sign.

BACK SOON.
