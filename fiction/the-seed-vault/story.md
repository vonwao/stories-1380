# The Seed Vault

The drawer that should not have been warm was warm.

Mira noticed it with the back of her hand during the Tuesday inventory. Chamber Three was held at minus eighteen. The steel drawers, the floor, the scanner, even the zipper on her coat all had the same dry cold. Drawer 19-C felt like somebody's arm beneath a sleeve.

She touched it again.

"Oskar."

He was two rows away, reading labels through fogged glasses.

"Come here."

"If you make me start this row over, I'm claiming overtime."

"Touch this."

He took off one glove and put his fingers against the drawer.

His face changed.

"Don't open it."

"I wasn't."

"Your other hand is on the latch."

Mira moved her hand.

Oskar checked the wall gauge, then the portable sensor. Minus eighteen. He ran the sensor along the seams of the drawer.

"Minus six at the edge," he said.

"That's not possible."

"It is currently happening."

He called the control room.

The vault was built inside a mountain, above a town where the winter sun disappeared for weeks. Its official name included the words Global, Agricultural, and Continuity. Everybody who worked there called it the mountain.

The mountain held nearly a million seed accessions in foil packets, filed by institution, country, crop, and year. It had backup generators, duplicate cooling systems, armed doors, and a written procedure for a reindeer blocking the access road. The warm drawer was not in any of the quick-reference cards.

Oskar found the longer procedure on his tablet.

"Anomalous viability event," he read.

"That's for a sprout."

"Heat can mean respiration."

"Or a sensor fault."

"The sensor is in my hand."

He put his glove back on.

"We open under containment."

Mira looked down the row. Every drawer around 19-C held grain collected from countries that had survived wars, droughts, governments, and mergers with other countries. The labels had been printed by at least four generations of office machines.

"Who deposited it?" Oskar asked.

Mira already knew.

The accession label bore the name Veyra.

Veyra had been dissolved in 1992. Its eastern provinces became one country, its coast another, and a strip through the middle spent eleven years under an international name nobody living there used. The flag on the original deposit records no longer flew.

"First intake," Mira said. "The 1991 shipment."

"Wheat?"

"Mostly."

"Why mostly?"

"There are garden samples in the back."

Oskar looked at her.

"Domestic garden, unverified," she said.

"My favorite crop."

They closed the aisle and called Lise, the vault director.

Lise arrived wearing office shoes beneath a cold suit. She had worked in the mountain for twenty-two years and had never once been seen hurrying.

"Show me readings."

Oskar showed her.

"Camera."

He clipped a body camera to his coat.

"Containment case."

Mira brought the clear transport box. They set it beneath the drawer and opened the lid.

"Now," Lise said.

Oskar pulled 19-C.

The wheat packets lay in neat rows. At the back, three small foil pouches had split along their seams. Pale roots hung from them. Folded green leaves pressed against the steel above.

No one spoke.

A drop of water formed on one leaf and fell into the transport case.

Oskar said, "Close the chamber."

"Sample first," Mira said.

"No."

"We need to know what it is."

"We remove the whole drawer."

"The roots are attached."

"Then we cut them."

Mira reached toward the nearest pouch.

Oskar caught her wrist.

"No."

"I'm wearing gloves."

"Wrong gloves."

Lise raised one hand.

They stopped.

"The entire drawer goes to quarantine," she said. "Seal it. We decide upstairs."

Mira and Oskar lowered the packets, roots and all, into the case. One leaf caught against the drawer lip. Mira freed it with one finger.

It was soft through her glove.

The warmth went out of 19-C as soon as the last pouch left it.

They carried the case through two air locks and into a concrete room beside the seed laboratory. Oskar started a contamination sheet. Lise called the phytosanitary office on the mainland.

Mira stood at the glass.

Under the quarantine lights, the seedlings began to unfold.

"You know the procedure," Lise said behind her.

"Sequence, pathology, viability."

"Not for unexplained germination in the cold chain."

Anomalous growth could introduce fungus or bacteria into the chambers. It could warm neighboring accessions. If it could spread, it could destroy the thing the vault was built to protect.

The instruction was simple: document, isolate, incinerate.

"We have until Friday," Mira said.

"We have until results come back or Friday, whichever comes first."

"What if it is unique?"

"Every accession in this building is unique to someone."

"This country doesn't exist."

"Which means there is no current depositor to authorize testing."

"Or destruction."

Lise looked at her.

"The deposit agreement authorizes us to protect the collection."

"From a plant."

"Seeds are plants delayed. Delay is the entire business."

Oskar was typing at the quarantine terminal.

"Temperature is rising in the case," he said.

Lise nodded. "Move it to negative pressure."

They put the clear case inside a sealed cabinet. A fan came on.

The leaves shook.

Mira had been seven when her grandmother Danica put a bitter green slice on her tongue and watched her chew.

"Good?" Danica asked.

It was not good. It tasted of cucumber skin, aspirin, and a coin.

Mira said yes because Danica had carried the plate from the kitchen like a gift.

The plant grew in a wooden box outside the apartment window. Danica called it Veyran melon. She saved the seeds on newspaper and stored them in a tea tin. After she died, Mira's mother cleared the apartment in a weekend. The tea tin was never found.

Mira had not thought about the melon for years.

The leaves inside the case were the same shape. Or close. Memory was generous with shapes.

She did not mention it.

That afternoon, the lab took root scrapings and a piece of leaf through the cabinet gloves. The fungal screen was negative. The bacterial culture needed two days. The genetic reference search returned no exact match.

"Cucurbit family," Oskar said. "Probably."

"Melon."

"Possibly."

"Bitter melon?"

"There are many bitter melons."

"From Veyra?"

"There is almost nothing in the reference database from Veyra."

Mira went back to the original accession papers.

The 1991 shipment had arrived in military crates packed with newspaper. Most packets carried typed labels from the Veyran agricultural ministry. The garden samples were in folded foil with handwriting.

Packet 19-C-44:

> D. Vukic, west window, third-floor flat. Fruit small, green. Keeps poorly. Seed from mother.

Mira read it three times.

Her grandmother's name had been Danica Vukic.

There could have been more than one. There must have been more than one.

She photographed the card and called her mother.

Elena answered from a supermarket.

"I need you to look at a picture."

"Now?"

"Yes."

"I'm holding fish."

"Put down the fish."

"It's wrapped."

Mira sent the accession card.

Her mother was quiet.

"Where did you get that?"

"At work."

"Why does work have Mama's writing?"

"You think it's hers."

"That D, yes. She made it like a ladder."

"Did she ever send seeds to the ministry?"

Elena laughed.

"She hated the ministry."

"Maybe somebody collected them."

"They collected everything in those years."

"The plant is growing."

The supermarket noise dropped away.

"What plant?"

"The melon."

"That horrible thing?"

"You remember it."

"I remember the smell. She boiled it when your father had his stomach trouble. He threatened to recover in the street."

"She said it tasted like home."

"She said many things."

"It didn't?"

"Home did not taste good, Mira."

Someone near Elena asked a question about fish. She answered, then came back.

"Your grandmother grew it because her mother grew it. She also complained every time it fruited. Too bitter. Too many vines. Little white flies. She tried to give it away and nobody wanted it."

"But she kept the seeds."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Ask her."

"I can't."

"Then don't make her simpler because she's dead."

Mira looked through the quarantine glass.

One vine had reached the wall of the cabinet and curled against it.

"Can you come here?"

"To the mountain?"

"If they let me grow it."

"Do they let you grow plants at the seed vault?"

"Not generally."

"Then this seems like the wrong place."

The next morning, Lise scheduled the incineration for Friday at nine.

The pathogen screens were clear. That did not change the decision. The plant had germinated at minus eighteen with no water and no light. It had heated a steel drawer from inside. Nobody could say why or whether another seed would do it.

"We can move one to the greenhouse," Mira said.

Lise sat at the end of the conference table. Oskar sat across from Mira with the test results in front of him.

"The greenhouse is outside containment," Lise said.

"There is a sealed growth cabinet."

"Rated for known material."

"All material is unknown before we test it."

"Not like this."

"The screens are clean."

"The screens cover things we know to screen for."

Mira turned to Oskar. "You said it's probably a melon."

"I said cucurbit."

"You know what it is."

"I know what it resembles."

"You're hiding behind vocabulary."

"Vocabulary is what keeps a guess from becoming a shipment."

Lise closed the folder.

"This vault does not own these seeds. We hold them. You do not own them either."

"My grandmother deposited them."

"Maybe. That gives you less room to act privately, not more."

"The depositor is gone."

"A country is not gone because its name changed."

"Who do we return it to?"

"Not your coat pocket."

Mira felt her face grow hot.

Lise had seen it.

"Did you remove material?"

"No."

"Did you intend to?"

Oskar looked at her.

"No," Mira said.

Lise waited.

"Not anymore."

The meeting ended.

Oskar followed Mira into the equipment corridor.

"Not anymore?"

"You grabbed my wrist."

"That was Tuesday."

"Then no."

"You have been looking at access logs."

"For the greenhouse."

"The greenhouse has cameras."

"I know."

"I am saying that because I don't want you to lose your job while believing you're being secret."

Mira stopped.

"Help me request an exception."

"Lise said no."

"The emergency committee can override her."

"It meets next month."

"Ask for a special meeting."

"For one plant?"

"For an accession from a dissolved depositor with no duplicate anywhere."

"And unexplained heat."

"Include it."

Oskar rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"My first seed-bank job had a fungus outbreak. We lost four hundred and six accessions. Not seeds. Accessions. Whole local lines. The director kept one unusual sample alive because it might be important. It was important. It contained the fungus."

"This one tested negative."

"For that fungus."

"So burn everything unusual."

"No. Keep millions of unusual things asleep. That's the work."

Mira looked through the small window in the quarantine door.

"It's not asleep."

"I noticed."

That night, she stayed after the last shuttle.

At eleven, only Mira, Oskar, and the control operator were in the mountain. Oskar was in Chamber One repairing a label reader.

Mira entered quarantine with a key from the emergency cabinet. The access system logged her name and the time.

She moved one seedling into a sealed transport cylinder. It had two leaves and a root curled beneath it. Through the plastic it looked too small to have caused anything.

The incinerator room was at the end of the service corridor. The greenhouse exit was the other way.

Mira stood at the crossing.

The cylinder warmed her hands.

Oskar came through the air lock carrying a screwdriver.

He looked at the cylinder.

"Put it back."

"I logged in."

"That doesn't make it allowed."

"No."

"The control operator has seen the door alarm."

"Then we don't have long."

"For what?"

"Help me get it into the sealed growth cabinet."

"I told you what happened at my bank."

"And I heard you."

"Did you?"

"Yes. Four hundred and six. This isn't simple for you either."

He looked down the corridor toward the incinerator.

"If we move it, the greenhouse shuts down," he said. "Every other project comes out. Full negative pressure. Nobody enters without suits."

"Okay."

"The growing cycle takes months."

"Okay."

"They suspend both of us."

"You can report me."

"I already didn't."

The control operator's voice came over the speaker.

"Quarantine corridor, identify status."

Oskar pressed the wall button.

"Unscheduled transfer," he said.

"Authorization?"

Oskar looked at Mira.

"Pending."

He released the button.

"That was a terrible answer," Mira said.

"Move."

They took the service lift to the greenhouse.

The mountain greenhouse was a low concrete room with twelve sealed growth cabinets. Inside, young wheat, rice, beans, and wild grasses stood beneath white lights. Oskar shut down Cabinets One through Eleven. Fans stopped one by one.

"People are going to hate us," he said.

"I know."

"The rice team especially."

They placed the Veyran seedling in Cabinet Twelve.

Its first leaf lifted toward the light.

Lise arrived fourteen minutes later, still in office shoes. She looked at the empty cylinder, the sealed cabinet, and the two of them.

"Badges," she said.

Mira gave hers over. Oskar hesitated, then gave his.

"The incineration remains scheduled."

"It's in a rated cabinet," Mira said.

"The whole greenhouse is now quarantine."

"Yes."

"Eleven active projects stopped."

"Yes."

"Two visiting research teams will have to fly home."

Mira did not say yes again.

Lise turned to Oskar.

"You knew better."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked at Cabinet Twelve.

"Because it was already awake."

At nine Friday morning, the incinerator burned the two remaining plants and every other packet from drawer 19-C.

Cabinet Twelve stayed on.

Lise had called an emergency meeting of the scientific committee at six. The committee did not forgive the unauthorized transfer. It also would not order the destruction of the last living sample while it remained sealed and pathogen screens were incomplete.

Mira and Oskar were suspended from vault access. The greenhouse became a restricted quarantine zone. A camera feed went to a laboratory on the mainland, where people who had never met Danica Vukic watched the plant.

Mira watched from home.

The vine climbed.

It flowered.

Several flowers fell without fruit. Oskar sent Mira a message each time.

> another failure

Then:

> one holding

The fruit was small, green, and ridged.

It took four months to ripen.

By then, twelve laboratories had tested the plant. Nobody found a pathogen. Nobody explained the warm drawer.

The vault did not give Mira her old access back. Lise allowed her and Elena into the greenhouse as deposit-family observers, a title invented for the visit.

They wore full suits.

Oskar met them inside. He had returned to work two weeks earlier under a final warning. He did not mention that Mira's appeal was still pending.

The fruit lay on a steel tray.

"That's it?" Elena asked.

"There were only two," Oskar said. "The other is for seed."

"Of course it is."

Mira cut the fruit.

The smell was green and sharp. For a moment she was seven at the apartment table, with Danica watching.

She handed her mother a slice.

Elena bit it. Her face folded.

"Terrible," she said.

"The same?"

Elena chewed.

"No."

Mira tasted hers. Bitter first. Then something dry and almost sweet underneath.

"Not the same," Elena said again.

She took the knife and cut herself another slice.
