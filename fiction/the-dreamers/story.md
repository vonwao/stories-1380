# The Dreamers — Episode 1

## The Community Hour

The music ended and nobody moved for a while. Twenty-some chairs in a circle, coats still on because the hall's heating only did so much, and at the front, on a small table with a white cloth, a framed photograph with a white ribbon on the corner. Zenka let the silence sit as long as she wanted. Then everyone opened their eyes.

"So. Who has something to report today?"

Jacob raised his hand a little way, the way people do when they have been waiting. "Last night I was in the mountain town again. The same one as last week. I was on the hiking path, the one that forks off the main road, down by the bridge. There was a market going on that made no sense, and my sister was there, although she looked like somebody else — you know how it is. A lot was happening. And then I came around the bend at the river and I saw it. The pink daisy. Suddenly it was there, by the water."

"A pink daisy." Zenka looked around the circle, unhurried. "We know what that means. Yes?" Nobody answered; nobody needed to. "Did anything happen after?"

"I walked toward it. The market was gone by then, it was just the river. I got close enough to see it clearly. It was growing where nothing else grew, straight out of the stones. And that's where it ended."

"Good. Thank you, Jacob." She wrote nothing down; she never wrote anything down. "Mattias. Do you have anything to share?"

Mattias looked at his hands. "Well. I don't know."

"You know."

"I was in Turkey. It looked different, but it was my uncle's house. I saw him sleeping." He stopped, and started again. "And the tall one stood beside him. Looking at him. And my uncle started turning pale, and he was having trouble breathing—"

"Did you stand strong, or did you accept it?"

"It was a horrible feeling. I was so afraid. I was standing at the door, and I was small, and I wanted to do something and I couldn't—"

"Mattias. STOP." Not loud. But the room went very still. "Where are you now? What do you feel now?"

"I still feel the fear. I'm afraid that something will happen to my uncle."

"We do not accept it. No." She let that stand for a moment. "But it is really up to you. You have to decide it for yourself. Do you accept it, Mattias?"

He shook his head, and she watched him until the head-shake became words. "No. I don't accept it."

"Friends, this is such an important lesson, right here today. Remember what Emanuel said. One thought can ruin everything. We have to be strong, because a great deal is happening right now — I don't have to tell you everything that is happening." She turned back to Mattias, and her voice, without changing at all, became something you could rest on. "Mattias, I'm sending you home with Lenore. You'll tune in together tonight and make use of her lovely company. And you don't sleep until you control the dark storm in your mind. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"But before we finish today, I want to give you another report, from Russia. There has been a big turnaround in Omsk. The fighting men who had a foothold in the town have left, and it has been peaceful now for several days. Sacha says the weather has been beautiful for the yearly festival, and they harvested some lovely vegetables. And this also is a lesson for us. Yes?"

Murmurs went around; someone on the far side of the circle said thank God in a language Mattias didn't place.

"I feel the divine energy now," Mattias said, surprising himself by speaking. "But I'm still shaken up. By Anton."

A small movement went through the circle, everyone at once and nobody in particular. Zenka did not move at all.

"You are new, so I will answer this for you once more. Anton is in the circle. But on the other side. We all know this. Yes?" And then, as if it were the most natural thing in the world: "Thomas. Will you come up and share your report from last week again? Some of us were not here that day."

Thomas stood and went to the front and stood beside the photograph, hands folded, a man who had told this before and would tell it exactly the same way every time.

"Of course. Hello, friends. I'm happy to share it again. It was quite an experience. I was up on the mountain. There was something in the sky, like birds, circling, and it drew me — I walked toward these birds. And then I saw him. I saw Anton. He was standing near the edge of a cliff, and the dark things were circling above him, around him. I felt he was in distress, so I called out to him. And I believe he heard me, because he looked over in my direction. But he couldn't see me. So I sent him all the good energy from my heart."

Nobody in the circle moved.

"Then he jumped. Off the cliff, on his own. He didn't fall, and nothing pushed him. He jumped. I ran to the edge, and when I looked over, it was ocean. Deep blue ocean."

"Did you see anything else?" Zenka asked, though she knew the answer. "Rocks? Anything he could have been caught on?"

"No rocks. Just the ocean. And then I saw, very clearly, a boat. And two divers going into the water after him."

"Yes." Zenka said it to the whole circle. "Those divers are from the circle."

"I woke up after that, and I felt an incredible peace. I felt that he had gone home. That he was with friends."

"Immediately rescued, and welcomed to the circle, on the other side." Zenka's eyes were shining. "It is so wonderful."

She stood, which was how the hour always ended.

"And with that, we will close. Let's keep the divine peace this week, and hold on to everything good we have received today. Tune in — at night, and again in the morning, before the world gets to you. And pay attention to what you find growing where nothing grows."

The chairs scraped; coats were buttoned; somebody's thermos went around. Lenore was already beside Mattias with his scarf, as though the assignment had been hers before it was announced. At the door Zenka put her hand on his arm and said it quietly, only to him.

"Mattias. Tonight, when you dream, you will see a friend there. Follow him. Stay close to him."

## Night Work

Lenore's kitchen smelled like dill and old wood, and she would not let Mattias help with anything.

"Sit. Drink your water." She set the two glasses down and lowered herself into the chair across from him, and turned on the little speaker, the same recording from the community hour, strings, very quiet. "Don't try to sleep tonight. Sleep isn't your job. Your job is the state you fall asleep in."

"And if I see it again?"

"Then it will see you standing." She closed her eyes and turned her palms up on the table, and after a moment he did the same. "No storm in this house tonight. My house, my weather."

They sat like that until the music ended, and then a while longer.

He was on the road above his uncle's village before he knew he was anywhere at all. Evening light, the dust holding it. Below him the roofs, the minaret, the walnut tree in his uncle's yard that his uncle had never once pruned out of a theory he defended at every family gathering.

A man was waiting at the fork where the road went down. Young, in a gray jacket, with his hands in his pockets, easy, like he'd only been there a minute. Mattias knew his face. He knew it from the framed photograph on the table at the front of the hall, the one with the white ribbon on the corner.

"You," Mattias said.

"Stay close to me," the friend said, and started down.

The village did what places do at night — it was his uncle's village and also somewhere in the mountains and also, for a few steps, the hallway of Mattias's school in Bremen, and the friend walked through all of it without hurrying, and where he walked it stayed his uncle's village. At the gate under the walnut tree he stopped.

"He's inside," the friend said. "It's there too. You know that."

"I couldn't move, last time."

"Last time you were alone." He looked at Mattias, and his eyes were calm the way deep water is calm. "It will get very loud in your chest. That's not you. Don't fight it, don't argue with it. You have one job. Look at your uncle. Only at him."

The room was the room from before. His uncle small in the bed, the blanket rising too little and too slowly. Standing at the head of the bed, the tall one, patient as furniture. The cold came off it like a door open to winter, and the loudness started in Mattias's chest, exactly as promised, the whole body wanting to be small, wanting to be at the door again, wanting to accept.

He looked at his uncle.

He looked at his uncle and remembered him at Selin's wedding with his jacket off, carrying two nephews, one on each shoulder, shouting that he was a tram and this was the route. Remembered the walnut theory. Remembered the way he said Mattias's name wrong on purpose, Matti, little Matti, come eat.

The breathing deepened. One breath, then another, unhurried, like a man rolling over in good sleep. The cold went out of the room the way evening goes out of a field — no moment you could point to, and then gone. When Mattias finally looked at the head of the bed there was nothing standing there, and the window was just a window.

The friend waited by the gate. Where the path met the road, growing out of the dust where nothing grew, there was a small pink daisy, and Mattias, who had sat through eleven community hours and heard the reports, stood very still in front of it for a long time.

"Tell them what you saw," the friend said. "All of it. In order. Exactly."

The kitchen again, morning, dill and old wood. Lenore already at the stove. He was still finding the edges of being awake when his phone went off against the table — his mother, the call starting mid-sentence the way she always started, in Turkish, too fast, and he had to ask her twice to slow down, and then he sat there holding the phone away from his ear, looking at nothing, while she said it again.

His uncle was asking for breakfast. He wanted the chair moved to the window. He wanted to sit in the sun.

Lenore didn't turn around. "Well?"

"I have something to report," Mattias said.
