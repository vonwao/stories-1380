# The Coffee Shop

In the dream my father has learned to make a flat white, which he never did in life, and he makes mine without being asked.

The dream is always the same building. It is the house on Lindholt Road where I grew up, the one I have not stood inside in nine years, except in the dream it is not a house anymore. Someone has knocked out the wall between the kitchen and the front room and put in a long counter of pale wood, and there are six small tables, and a chalkboard with the prices in my father's blocky engineer's capitals. The window where my mother kept her violets is now the order window, steamed at the corners. It smells like a real place. That is the part I cannot explain to anyone. It smells like roasted beans and milk going hot and, underneath, the specific mildew of that particular basement, the one we never fixed, and the dream does not flinch from including it.

He stands behind the counter in an apron. He is younger than when I last saw him and older than my childhood, somewhere in the middle, a man of maybe fifty who has made peace with his hands. He nods when I come in. He does not say the thing. In nine years of not speaking I had built an entire architecture around the things he would say if forced, the defenses and the dismissals, and the dream simply declines to use any of it. He froths the milk. He slides the cup across. "Careful," he says. "It's hot." That is the whole of it. That is the most we ever say.

I wake up calm in a way my waking life has not managed since the funeral. I did not go to the funeral. I want to be honest about that here, because everything else depends on it.

My sister called me when he was dying and I said I would come and then I did not come, and he died on a Tuesday in March without me, and I have spent fifteen months understanding that there is no version of the future in which I fix this, because the other party to the repair is in the ground at St. Brigid's and the dream is the only place he still pours coffee. I am not a man who believes the dead send messages. I want that on the record too. I think the brain is a janitor working the night shift, going through the day's drawers, and mine had simply decided that the cheapest way to give me an hour of rest was to build a café and put my father in it where he could not say anything that would start the fight again.

So I knew the dream was about me. I knew it was not him. I am not stupid about this.

I went to Lindholt Road anyway.

I told myself I was driving past. The new people had repainted it gray and there was a child's bicycle on its side in the yard and the violet window had a roller shade pulled down. I sat across the street for forty minutes and the building did not turn into a coffee shop, of course it did not, it sat there being a house with someone else's life in it, and I felt foolish and then I felt the particular grief of a place that has gone on perfectly well without you, which is a colder grief than I expected. I drove home. The next week I went back.

I do not know what I thought I was going to be handed.

The third time, the new owner came out. A man about my age, in socks, holding a coffee mug, which I noticed with a lurch that I tried to keep off my face. He was not unfriendly. He asked, in the careful way you ask, whether he could help me. I said I used to live here. His face did the thing faces do, softened and got wary at once. He said his wife had found a box in the crawlspace when they redid the basement, and they hadn't known how to find anyone, and would I want it.

It was a shoebox. Inside were the brackets and clips of a hobby I had forgotten my father had, the little brass fittings for the model railroad he kept in the basement, the one I was forbidden to touch and touched constantly. And folded under the fittings, a sheet of his graph paper, and on it, in the blocky capitals, a track plan. A station. A platform. And in the corner, small, where he labeled everything: a building marked CAFÉ.

I want to tell you this is where the dream came from, that I saw this plan as a boy and buried it and my janitor brain dug it up. That is almost certainly true. He had drawn a café into the world he built downstairs and called it nothing fancier than CAFÉ, and forty years later my sleeping mind had borrowed the word. There is no mystery here. I keep saying that because I need it to be true.

But it does not account for the apron.

He never wore an apron in his life. He wore the same gray cardigan to the workbench and burned a hole in it once with the soldering iron and refused to throw it out. The man in the dream wears a clean blue apron and makes my drink without being asked, and I did not give him that. I have gone over it. I gave him the building and the word and the basement smell, all of it scavenged, and somewhere in the assembly my mind decided, without consulting me, that the version of him I needed was a man who would feed me and warn me the cup was hot.

That is the part that undoes me. Not that he came back. That my own brain, left alone in the dark, built me a father who was gentle. It had the materials all along. It just never offered.

I took the box. I thanked the man in his socks. I did not tell him what was in it because it was not his to be sad about.

I still have the dream, less often now, which I am told is how it goes. Last night he was wiping down the counter at closing and the chalkboard was already erased and he looked up and for a second I was sure he was going to say it, the thing, the apology or the accusation, the sentence the whole estrangement had been waiting on for nine years and then for fifteen months more.

He said, "We're out of the oat milk. Is regular okay."

I said regular was fine. It is the closest we will ever come, and in the dream I let it be enough, and then I wake up, and it is morning, and it is not.
