# Ninety Minutes

The deal was simple: sit through a ninety-minute presentation at a resort near Kissimmee, and they give you three nights on a cruise to Cozumel. Ana found it through her cousin, who had done it twice and called it "the easiest money in Florida, you just have to not be weak."

I want to say up front that I prepared. I read the forums. I learned the stages the way you'd learn a fire drill: the tour, the lifestyle questions, the first offer, the manager, the "today only" board, and the last guy, the one they call the closer, who is friendly again because his job is to make sure you leave loving them even though you said no. I printed an exit script and folded it into my shirt pocket. Ana watched me do this and said, "It's a timeshare, not a hostage exchange," and I said, "It's both," and I stand by that.

Our salesman was Rick. Fifties, golf tan, the handshake of a man who has moved a lot of product and buried a lot of feelings. He showed us a model unit with a kitchen island bigger than our first apartment, and he kept saying "when you own," never "if" — "when you own, this is your closet," like the future was already notarized.

I ran my script. I asked about maintenance fees, resale value, the secondary market. Rick answered everything honestly, which the forums had warned me about. The honest ones are the dangerous ones.

At minute forty they brought the manager, Dale, who had a laminated sheet and a theory about inflation. At minute sixty they brought a whiteboard with today's price crossed out twice, each cross-out signed, like a treaty. Ana squeezed my knee under the table, which was our signal, although by then I couldn't remember if it meant "hold" or "we're actually considering this," and that is how they get you — not through greed, through fatigue and a beautiful kitchen island.

"We're not buying anything today," I said, for the fourth time, and Dale smiled and left to get "one more figure," which the forums call the drop, and Rick and Ana and I sat there in the model living room, in the quiet, three people around a fake family's coffee table.

And then Rick looked at his own hands and stopped being Rick.

"You folks are a no," he said. "You were a no in the parking lot. I've been doing this eleven years; I can tell from how people get out of the car."

I said we were sorry. I actually apologized, to a timeshare salesman, in a room designed to bankrupt me.

"Don't be. Nos are fine. Nos are the job." He glanced at the door Dale had gone through. "You know what the hard part is? It's not the nos. It's that I say 'when you own' about four hundred times a week, and my daughter asked me at dinner what I'd do when I own, me, and I couldn't think of one thing. Eleven years of when-you-own and I rent in Davenport." He laughed, and it wasn't bitter, which somehow made it worse. "Anyway. Dale's going to come back with a number and a story about a cancellation. The number's real, the cancellation isn't."

Dale came back with a number and a story about a cancellation from Ohio. We said no. He shook our hands warmly, the way you'd congratulate someone, and passed us to the closer, who offered us a smaller thing, a "trial membership," which the forums call the exit ramp, and we said no to that too, and then it was minute eighty-eight and a woman at a desk validated our parking and handed over the cruise voucher like it was a subpoena.

Rick walked us out. He wasn't supposed to; I could tell because he checked the lobby first.

"Do me a favor," he said at the doors. "The survey they email you. Say I was terrible. Say I barely tried." He shook my hand. "If I'm too good with the nos, they make me train people, and the trainers don't get floor commissions."

Then he turned around and walked back toward the model unit, where a couple in matching Disney shirts was just getting out of a golf cart, and I heard him say, from twenty feet, in a voice with all its paint back on, "Folks, wait till you see this kitchen."

We did the cruise in March. It was fine. The boat had a kitchen island too, in the buffet, and I stood at it one night holding a small plate of shrimp, doing the math on what three free nights had actually cost, not in money. I'd said no ninety times to a man who was better at his job than I have ever been at anything, and the only thing he asked me for, the entire time, was to tell his bosses he was worse.

I filled out the survey on the boat's wifi. One star. "Salesman seemed distracted. Barely tried." Ana read it over my shoulder and said I'd just written the man a love letter, and I said yes, one star, the best one I had.
