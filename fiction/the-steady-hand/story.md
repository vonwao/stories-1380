# The Steady Hand

There are eleven videos of the worst thing that ever happened at our pool, and mine is the best one.

That is not my judgment. That is what the lawyer said, a young guy with a paper coffee cup who came to the clubhouse on a Thursday and watched all eleven on his laptop with the sound off, twice. Ramírez's is shaky. The Hoffmann kid filmed vertical and mostly got the umbrella. Sondra from 214 started late, after the first scream, which makes everything look like it came from nowhere. Mine starts eleven seconds before anything happens.

"Yours is the one," he said. "You held steady."

I want to put down what the afternoon was actually like, because the videos don't have the heat in them. Saturday, end of May, the water too warm to cool anyone off. Kids playing the game where the noodle is a crocodile. Diego from the second floor asleep on his chest with his mouth open, his two boys and my two in the shallow end, and the woman — I knew her face, not her name, she parks the silver Camry across two spots — already standing at the pool edge with her sandals on, which nobody does, telling the kids to stop splashing. Not asking. Telling, in that voice that's really for the adults.

My son says the crocodile game needs splashing. He's eight. It's a fair point, delivered fairly.

She calls him something I'm not going to write down.

And here is where my memory and my video agree, because I've checked: my hand was already up. Before Diego woke, before anyone yelled. Some part of me had read the woman's sandals and her two parking spots and had gotten the phone out and framed the shot, and when she leaned down over the water toward my son, I did not move toward her. I moved two steps left, where the light was better and the umbrella pole wasn't in the way.

Diego came up off the chair like a man who hadn't been asleep at all. What he said, she talked over. What she said back had his boys in it. Then the slap — flat, loud, a sound like a tarp snapping, and eleven phones already up around that pool like candles at a vigil, and in my frame you can see four of the other phones, which the lawyer says is useful. It establishes context, he says.

He wants the file by Friday. The original, he said, straight off the phone, untouched — the first thing her lawyer does is ask what you edited, and edits are how videos die. The HOA wants it never to have existed; the property manager sent an email with the subject line "Community Standards Reminder" that does not mention the slap, the woman, or the child she leaned over, my child, the one whose name I have not put in this account the way I did not put down my phone.

I've watched my video maybe forty times now, nights, with the earbuds in so my wife doesn't ask. It really is the best one. The framing is calm. The exposure rides the shift when the cloud moved. Around second thirty, there's a sound in it, close to the microphone, under everything — I had to turn it all the way up to be sure what it was.

I will come back to the sound. Things should go in the order they happened. Order is half of what a video is.

Three days before the file was due, Diego knocked on my door holding a covered plate, which is how he apologizes for needing something. His boys hadn't been back to the pool. His family moved in over the winter and there were still boxes in his living room, and his wife had stopped opening them, because the lease comes up in August and there was no point finding out what was in them until they knew. He set the plate on my table and turned it a quarter turn, like he was tuning it.

"You heard what she's telling people," he said. "She told the police I grabbed her arm. That the slap was to get free of me."

"Nobody who was there believes that."

"Nobody who was there decides it. There's a hearing in October." He looked at his hands, then put them under the table. "They say yours starts before. That you have the whole beginning."

"I have it."

"The beginning is everything, hermano. The beginning is where I never touch her. The beginning is her, over the water, over your boy." He shook his head, slow, and then he did the thing I have not been able to put down until now: he smiled at me with real love. "Everyone at that pool lost their head except you. I go up like a bull. Eleven phones in the air, everybody screaming. And you were already working. Calm. God puts one serious man at every fire."

"My boys don't ask to swim anymore," he said, quieter. "They ask if the lady is still there. Like she lives in the pool now."

I told him the lawyer would have the file by Friday. He stood and hugged me, and the hug went one second past comfortable, the way gratitude does when a man has nowhere to put it.

That night my wife watched me take the earbuds out of the nightstand drawer and asked how many times that made.

"It has to be right before it goes in," I said.

"It went in the day you filmed it. You're not watching it for the lawyer." She sat on her side of the bed with her back to me. "He asked me something today."

"Who did?"

"Your son. He asked if he looks scared in it." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed. "Not what the woman said. Not what happens to Diego. He wants to know how he looks. I told him he looks brave. I've never seen it. Does he look brave?"

"He looks small," I said, which was true, and was not what she asked.

She turned off the light, and out of the dark she said, "You were right there," and was asleep, or done, before I could decide which way she meant it.

The woman from the silver Camry caught me at the mailboxes on Wednesday evening. Sandals again. Up close she was smaller than the video makes her, and pleasant, which I want on the record because it was the worst part.

"You're the one with the good video," she said. Not a question. "Eleven of them, I'm told. Like a film festival."

I said the mail had been slow this week. I am not proud of that sentence.

"The board feels a hearing helps nobody. My husband agrees." Her husband chairs the landscaping committee, which sounds like nothing and is how everything in this building actually gets decided. "These things used to be settled between neighbors. Now everything has a production around it."

"There's a child in it," I said. "Two families."

"That family's lease is up in August," she said, in the voice you'd use for a parking rule. "By fall this is a story people tell wrong at the barbecue. Unless somebody's video keeps it alive."

Then she stepped closer, and her voice went kind, and kindness on her was like the sandals at the pool edge — a thing worn where it didn't belong.

"I remember you from that day. Everyone shrieking, and you stepped sideways. For the light, I think." She smiled like we had history. "You and I were the only two people at that pool who didn't lose our heads."

She wished my family a good evening, carried her mail to the Camry, and pulled out of her two spots without checking the mirror, the way you drive when you have never once been stopped.

I went home and did the thing I had been circling for two weeks. I put the file on the computer, dragged the volume all the way up, and sat with the earbuds in, because I needed her to be wrong.

The slap is at second twenty-eight. It still sounds like a tarp snapping. At second twenty-nine, my son turns and looks at the camera. Not at the woman. Not at Diego. At the camera, because the camera is where his father is.

At second thirty, close to the microphone, under all of it, a man's voice says, quietly, "Got it."

It is my voice. It is not shocked and it is not afraid. It is the voice of a man catching keys.

There is something else, once you know to listen. My breathing is on the file too, close and even, the whole ninety seconds, and it never changes once. Not when she leans over the water. Not at the slap. The lawyer watched this video twice with the sound off and gave that a word, and the word was steady.

I can tell you the first feeling, because I have now had it forty times and it does not improve. It is not shame. Shame arrives second, and arrives worse for the wait. The first feeling, every time, at second thirty, is pride.

The editing program has a small speaker icon. Two clicks and a video has no sound, and never admits it lost anything. I sat a long time with those two clicks between me and Friday, and with the lawyer's voice saying original, untouched, edits are how videos die, and with Diego's boxes that nobody would open until August.

I would like to write that I sent it whole for Diego's boys, or because a true thing should stay whole. What I can write is that I sent it whole.

The lawyer's office is a suite above a tax place. Same paper cup, different day. He asked if it was the original and I said off the phone, untouched, and he copied it over and then, which I had not planned for, watched it right there in front of me. This time he put in earbuds.

At what could only have been second thirty, he looked up at me over the laptop, one earbud still in. I stood there and let him look.

"There's audio on yours," he said.

"Yes."

He was quiet for a moment, a young guy deciding what was and wasn't his business. "Ambient sound reads honest," he said finally. "A phone that hears everything is a phone nobody coached." He closed the laptop. "With the beginning, her story's finished. You held steady."

At the door he told me her lawyer gets a copy Monday. Everyone hears everything, he said. That's the system working.

So there is now a version of October where that video plays in a hearing room at full volume through good speakers, with the woman at one table and Diego at the other and my wife, who has said she is coming, two rows behind me. The lawyer says most of these settle before it gets that far. I have started hoping for a settlement in a way I keep out of my face at dinner.

On Saturday, over cereal, my son asked whether the kids at school would get to see the video. I said no. He pushed the flakes around his bowl for a while and then asked if he could see it — not the whole thing, just his part. I said no to that too. He nodded like a man accepting a settlement.

On Sunday my son fell off his bike in the parking loop. The ordinary way — a pedal, a curb, no villain anywhere in it. I was at the mailboxes, and I ran, and I want it on the record that I ran.

He was sitting up with blood on his chin, holding the cry in his chest the way he does, deciding how bad it was. When I reached him he didn't look at my face. He looked past it, at my hands.

"Did you get it?" he said.

My hands were empty. I picked him up with both of them.
