# The Steady Hand (partial)

There are eleven videos of the worst thing that ever happened at our pool, and mine is the best one.

That is not my judgment. That is what the lawyer said, a young guy with a paper coffee cup who came to the clubhouse on a Thursday and watched all eleven on his laptop with the sound off, twice. Ramírez's is shaky. The Hoffmann kid filmed vertical and mostly got the umbrella. Sondra from 214 started late, after the first scream, which makes everything look like it came from nowhere. Mine starts eleven seconds before anything happens.

"Yours is the one," he said. "You held steady."

I want to put down what the afternoon was actually like, because the videos don't have the heat in them. Saturday, end of May, the water too warm to cool anyone off. Kids playing the game where the noodle is a crocodile. Diego from the second floor asleep on his chest with his mouth open, his two boys and my two in the shallow end, and the woman — I knew her face, not her name, she parks the silver Camry across two spots — already standing at the pool edge with her sandals on, which nobody does, telling the kids to stop splashing. Not asking. Telling, in that voice that's really for the adults.

My son says the crocodile game needs splashing. He's eight. It's a fair point, delivered fairly.

She calls him something I'm not going to write down.

And here is where my memory and my video agree, because I've checked: my hand was already up. Before Diego woke, before anyone yelled. Some part of me had read the woman's sandals and her two parking spots and had gotten the phone out and framed the shot, and when she leaned down over the water toward my son, I did not move toward her. I moved two steps left, where the light was better and the umbrella pole wasn't in the way.

Diego came up off the chair like a man who hadn't been asleep at all. What he said, she talked over. What she said back had his boys in it. Then the slap — flat, loud, a sound like a tarp snapping, and eleven phones already up around that pool like candles at a vigil, and in my frame you can see four of the other phones, which the lawyer says is useful. It establishes context, he says.

He wants the file by Friday. The HOA wants it never to have existed; the property manager sent an email with the subject line "Community Standards Reminder" that does not mention the slap, the woman, or the child she leaned over, my child, the one whose name I have not put in this account the way I did not put down my phone.

I've watched my video maybe forty times now, nights, with the earbuds in so my wife doesn't ask. It really is the best one. The framing is calm. The exposure rides the shift when the cloud moved. Around second thirty, there's a sound in it, close to the microphone, under everything — I had to turn it all the way up to be sure what it was.
