# Fake Grandsons of the Palm Beaches

The company was called Grandsons of the Palm Beaches, although three of us were women and one of us was fifty-eight.

Marsha said grandson was a category of service, not a biological claim. She had said this to a lawyer and the lawyer had apparently stopped asking questions, so it became part of orientation.

There were nine rules in the binder.

Do not borrow money. Do not accept jewelry. Do not witness a will. Do not provide medication, transportation after dark, tax advice, medical advice, or password help. Never say you are family to a doctor, police officer, bank employee, or member of the clergy. Do not meet actual relatives without office approval. If a client asks you to move furniture, you may move one chair.

The last rule was underlined twice.

No attachment.

"How do you enforce that?" I asked.

Marsha looked up from the turkey sandwich she had brought into the conference room.

"Poorly."

That was my interview.

I started the following Tuesday at thirty-two dollars an hour plus mileage. I had been an actor for eleven years, if you count the years when I made most of my money installing kitchen cabinets. I did. The Screen Actors Guild did not.

The clients could choose from several packages. The basic package was two visits a month and a birthday call. Platinum included weekly visits, attendance at one holiday, light technology support, and a framed graduation picture of the grandson in a cap and gown. We used the same university lawn for everyone. Marsha kept four gowns in the supply closet.

"People buy the photo?" I asked.

"People buy six," she said.

She assigned me to Evelyn Osterman, eighty-six, Palm Beach, Tuesdays from two to four. The intake sheet said she liked chess, orchids, chopped liver, bankruptcy hearings, and men who arrived on time. It said not to mention her late husband unless she did. Under FAMILY BACKGROUND, somebody had typed:

> One grandson, Graham, New York. Financial adviser. Two visits last year. Relationship complicated.

Under MY ROLE:

> Daniel. Twenty-nine. Second-year law student in Boston. Mother is Evelyn's fictional daughter, Susan. Interested in land-use law. Allergic to shellfish.

"I'm not allergic to shellfish," I told Marsha.

"Daniel is."

"Why law school?"

"She picked it."

"Why Boston?"

"She thinks Florida makes young men soft."

Marsha handed me a folder with three pages of invented family history. There were Christmases in Connecticut, a broken wrist at Camp Laurel, and an Aunt Joanne who had once thrown a pie. The amount of detail worried me.

"Mrs. Osterman knows I'm not Daniel."

"Of course."

"Then why do I have to know where I broke my wrist?"

"Because she knows you know she knows. That doesn't mean she wants a lazy performance."

The first Tuesday I arrived at 1:56. A housekeeper let me into an apartment with more glass than walls and took me to a room overlooking the ocean. Mrs. Osterman sat at a card table playing chess against herself.

She was small and dressed in white. Her hair had the firmness of an architectural model.

"You're early," she said.

"Four minutes."

"That's early. Sit down, Daniel."

I sat.

She moved a pawn.

"How is Boston?"

"Cold."

"It's September."

"Boston starts early."

She looked at me for the first time. "Do you play?"

"Badly."

"Good. Graham plays well and takes forever."

We played. She beat me in twenty-one moves. On the twenty-second, after the game was over, she reached across and moved my rook back to where it had been six minutes earlier.

"That's where you lost."

"I thought it was the queen."

"The queen was the consequence. The rook was the mistake."

She wrote something in a little green notebook.

"Do you keep score?"

"Of things."

At three she asked me to open a jar of mustard. At three fifteen she told me about a bankruptcy hearing in Ohio where a man had tried to list his wife's parrot as a business asset. At three forty she asked if I had a girlfriend.

"No."

"Daniel does."

"That wasn't in the folder."

"I'm adding it."

"What's her name?"

"Think of one."

"Maya."

"Too pretty. You'll make her dull."

"Colleen."

"Now you're punishing her."

By the end of the visit, Daniel was dating a dental hygienist named Frances who raced small sailboats and disliked him for valid reasons.

Mrs. Osterman handed me twelve dollars for parking.

"I can expense it."

"Then expense it and keep the twelve."

"I'm not allowed to accept money."

"It's reimbursement."

"The company reimburses me."

"Then consider it damages for Boston."

I left the cash on the chessboard. She entered something in the green notebook.

Marsha called before I got to the bridge.

"What did you do?"

"Lost at chess."

"She requested you again."

"Is that unusual?"

"She fired the last one during the first visit."

"Why?"

"He won."

For the next four months, I went every Tuesday.

Mrs. Osterman never called me Micah, which was my real name. I never called her Evelyn. We had Daniel and Mrs. Osterman, and inside those names we got along.

The file Marsha had given me became useless within three weeks. Mrs. Osterman was not interested in Daniel's invented childhood. She was interested in his current trouble. Frances became more demanding. Law school became less certain. Daniel developed a professor who hated him and a landlord who stored rowing equipment in his bathroom. If I arrived without news, she complained.

"You can't have no trouble," she said. "You're twenty-nine."

Sometimes she gave advice. Most of it was bad.

"Never apologize to a landlord."

"Why?"

"It excites them."

She made me bring legal questions so she could answer them without qualifications. She was wrong about nearly everything and confident in different amounts. I began checking the answers afterward. One Tuesday she told me a person could legally keep any boat that drifted into his yard. I printed the salvage law and brought it the following week.

She read the first page, then put it under the leg of the chess table because the table had started to rock.

"Useful," she said.

Other things entered the routine.

She ate half a turkey sandwich at two thirty and gave me the other half without asking if I wanted it. She stole hotel soap, including from hotels she had not stayed at. She watched court hearings on a tablet with the volume too high. She hated orchids, despite what the intake sheet said. The orchids belonged to her husband. She kept them alive because he had expected her to kill them.

"Out of spite?" I asked.

"Spite is a kind of continuity."

Once a month I rotated the pots toward the glass. This did not count as furniture because each pot was smaller than a chair.

In November, she asked me to attend Thanksgiving.

"Platinum includes one holiday," she said.

"It has to be approved."

"Approve it."

"I don't approve things."

"Then you're not much of a lawyer."

Her daughter and son-in-law were flying down from Connecticut. Graham was coming from New York with his wife and two boys. Mrs. Osterman wanted me there from three until dessert.

"As Daniel?"

"Obviously."

"Does your family know?"

"They know I employ people."

"That wasn't the question."

"Then I don't care for the question."

Marsha refused.

We were in the same conference room where she had hired me. This time she had soup.

"Actual relatives are a risk," she said. "They see a younger person spending time with an older person. They see money leaving the estate, even if it's thirty-two dollars an hour. Then they call me and say undue influence."

"What if she tells them what the service is?"

"Then they say humiliation."

"She's asking me to come."

"Clients ask for things. That's why we have a binder."

"The package includes a holiday."

"A holiday where the family is informed and consents."

"Does anyone's family consent to this?"

Marsha ate a spoonful of soup.

"One family. They own a theatrical supply company."

I told Mrs. Osterman no.

She looked at the board. It was my move.

"Is that the company answer or the Daniel answer?"

"Both."

"There is no Daniel answer."

She had never said that before. It landed harder than it should have.

"Okay," I said.

"Micah, don't sulk. It's unattractive in both of you."

I looked up.

She moved one of her bishops, although it was still my turn.

"You left your parking receipt in the bathroom in October," she said. "Your name is on it. Also, Daniel never forgets to remove cabinet glue from his thumbnail."

I rubbed the thumbnail against my jeans.

"You knew."

"I hired a pretend grandson. I wasn't expecting Daniel Day-Lewis."

"That's a very specific joke."

"I know actors."

She did. In the 1960s she had worked as a production accountant for television commercials. She had kept that off the intake form because, she said, it was none of Marsha's business.

"Come for an hour," she said. "No Daniel. No law school. You can tell them you're the orchid man."

"You hate the orchids."

"Exactly. That gives you status."

I went.

I did not tell Marsha.

Thanksgiving at Mrs. Osterman's began with a catered turkey the size of a carry-on bag and two boys fighting over a charger. Her daughter, Lisa, greeted me as if we had met before and had both forgotten where. Her husband actually wanted to discuss orchids. I told him I mostly turned them toward windows.

Graham came in last, still finishing a phone call. He was around forty, taller than I expected, with the tired good posture of a man who wore expensive shoes in airports.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Micah. I help your grandmother with the plants."

Mrs. Osterman, from across the room, said, "He also loses at chess."

Graham looked at her, then at me.

"The grandson," he said.

His wife said his name quietly. He put his phone away.

"Can I talk to you outside?"

The balcony was windy and had no privacy. Everybody inside could see us through the glass.

"How much is she paying you?"

"Thirty-two dollars an hour. The agency pays mileage."

"Agency."

"Grandsons of the Palm Beaches."

He closed his eyes.

"That's the name?"

"Marsha says grandson is a category of service."

He laughed once, despite himself, then looked angry that I had made it happen.

"Has she given you anything?"

"Twelve dollars. I left it."

"Has she discussed her will?"

"No."

"Bank accounts?"

"No."

"Has she asked you to drive her anywhere?"

"No."

"Do you know she sent fourteen thousand dollars to a man who said he was calling from the Social Security Administration?"

I did not.

"Do you know she fell in this apartment last year and lay by that glass table for six hours because she wouldn't wear the button?"

I did not know that either.

"I am not some asshole grandson from a pamphlet," he said. "I have a job. I have children. My mother calls me when she can't get her to answer. I fly down. She tells everyone I visit twice a year because she doesn't count the trips where something is wrong."

Inside, Mrs. Osterman was cutting a slice of turkey for one of the boys and correcting the caterer.

"She said the relationship was complicated," I said.

"Did the agency put that in your little folder?"

"Yes."

"That's nice. Do they have a package where someone else feels guilty for you?"

I had nothing to say to that.

The door opened. Mrs. Osterman stood there holding the mustard.

"Micah, this is impossible. Graham, stop interrogating the staff. If either of you intends to be useful, the jar is stuck."

Graham took it from her. He tapped the lid against the stone counter, once on each side, and opened it.

"Your grandfather's method," she said.

"I know."

That was the first time I saw them look like family.

Dinner was worse before it got better. Graham asked what law school I attended. Mrs. Osterman told him to stop. Lisa asked how the business worked, genuinely curious, which somehow made it more embarrassing. One of the boys wanted to know if there were fake granddaughters. His mother told him this was not appropriate. He asked why.

Mrs. Osterman ate turkey and watched all of us.

"Micah comes on Tuesdays," she said finally. "You come when something is wrong. I would like some company when nothing is wrong."

"You could call me," Graham said.

"I do. You send me links."

"Because you ask me questions."

"I don't want the answer. I want to complain about the question."

"How am I supposed to know that?"

"You could learn."

He put down his fork.

"You could also answer the phone."

She looked away first.

Nothing was fixed at dinner. Graham still did not like me. Mrs. Osterman still refused the emergency button. The boys got pie on the white sofa. I stayed until dessert and then left before anyone could decide whether I belonged in the family photograph.

Marsha found out the next morning.

Graham called the office. He did not threaten to sue, which Marsha said was almost worse. He asked for contracts, background checks, and the company's insurance certificate. Marsha gave him all three and suspended me.

"I told you actual family is a risk."

"He had fair questions."

"They usually do."

"Are you firing me?"

"Mrs. Osterman refuses reassignment. Her daughter says Tuesdays have helped. Graham says the whole thing is grotesque."

"What do you say?"

Marsha looked tired. She took the binder from the shelf and opened it to the last rule.

"I say this rule is here because every other version we tried was longer."

She suspended me for thirty days and removed the holiday charge from Mrs. Osterman's account. I went back to cabinet work. On Tuesdays I kept checking the time around two.

At the end of the month, Graham called me.

"She's in the hospital," he said.

I sat down on an unopened box of drawer slides.

"What happened?"

"Pneumonia. She asked whether Daniel had passed land-use."

"He withdrew."

"Of course he did."

There was a pause.

"She wants you to come," he said.

"The agency suspended me."

"I know. Marsha explained attachment to me for forty-five minutes."

"She can do that."

"I'm not calling the agency."

Mrs. Osterman looked smaller in the hospital bed, but not softened. She complained about the television, the broth, and the placement of an IV pole that had no relationship to her work.

Graham sat by the window answering email. When I came in, he stood.

"Your shift," he said.

"I'm not on shift."

He nodded. "Right."

Mrs. Osterman opened her eyes.

"Daniel," she said.

"Micah."

"Not with that jacket."

I sat down.

She had made Graham bring the travel chess set from her apartment. The pieces were magnetic and too small for her hands. I moved for both of us according to her instructions. She tried the same rook trap from our first game. I saw it this time and moved around it.

"Finally," she said.

She beat me anyway.

After that, Tuesdays became unofficial.

Sometimes Graham paid me and sometimes he forgot. I stopped submitting mileage. Mrs. Osterman recovered enough to go home, then did not recover past that. By spring she had oxygen beside the chess table and a nurse who regarded all of us as weather.

Graham called more often. Mrs. Osterman still complained about the links. Once I arrived and found him on the sofa while she slept. We spoke quietly for almost an hour. He told me about the scam, the fall, his mother, his job, and three different assisted-living places Mrs. Osterman had rejected because, in her words, the ceilings encouraged death.

"She tells you things," he said.

"She makes things up with me."

"Maybe that's easier."

"Probably."

The last game we played took three visits. She slept between moves. On the third Tuesday, I arrived and the chessboard was put away.

She died Thursday morning.

I did not attend the funeral. That was not a rule. I just could not work out where to stand.

Two weeks later Graham came to my apartment carrying the chessboard under one arm and a paper grocery bag under the other. The bag held every hotel soap she had stolen in the last ten years.

"My mother says these are yours."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows."

He put the chessboard on my table. An envelope was taped underneath. It had MICAH written on it in Mrs. Osterman's square handwriting.

Inside was the original twelve dollars from parking and one page torn from the green notebook.

It was not a letter. It was a list of our games, dates and results. She had won forty-three. I had won two. One was marked QUESTIONABLE.

At the bottom she had written:

> Daniel was hopeless. Micah improved.

That was all.

Graham stood by the table. He had taken his jacket off but still held it over one arm.

"Do you play?" I asked.

"Not well."

"Good."

We set up the pieces.

He opened with his queen's pawn. Mrs. Osterman had hated that opening and called it a request for supervision. I almost told him. Then I let him make the mistake.

We played until the soaps started to smell up the room.
