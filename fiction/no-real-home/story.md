# No Real Home

I figured out what the depression was on a Tuesday, standing in a kitchen where the pans have labels.

Not my labels. I rent a room in a Somerville triple-decker that runs smoothly because everything in it belongs to somebody specific, and the somebody is never me. There is tape on the shelf that says DENNIS in block capitals, and the good cast-iron skillet lives under the counter in a spot I know and don't use, because using it means washing it and drying it and putting it back at an angle I once got wrong. I stood there holding a jar of lentils I had no real way to cook, and the sentence arrived whole, the way true sentences do: I'm not sad. I don't have a home. Those are different problems, and I'd been treating the wrong one.

I have an address. I've always had an address — I want to be fair to my own life here. What I don't have is a kitchen, and I've started to think those are the two different words for home, and the address is the one that doesn't matter.

Here's my test, and you can run it on yourself: in the place you live, can you cook for someone else without asking anyone's permission? Not reheat. Cook — the kind that takes three hours and makes the whole house smell like your position on the world, and leaves a mess you're entitled to clean up slowly. If yes, you have a home. If no, you have a place where you sleep and a very personal relationship with takeout.

By that test I have had, in fifty-three years across two continents, maybe three homes, and I only cooked my way through one of them.

My daughter calls on Wednesdays, from New York, usually while walking somewhere loudly.

"I'm in Boston Saturday," Lena said. "One night. We can do Legal's again."

Here is a number I'm not proud of. I have bought my daughter dinner in forty-one restaurants. I counted once, on a flight, with the seatback screen off. Forty-one restaurants, four states, two countries. In the same twenty-six years I have cooked for her twice, and one of those was grilled cheese during a power outage, which shouldn't count, and I count it anyway because the list is too short to leave anything off.

"Come here instead," I said. "I'll cook."

The walking noise stopped. "You'll cook."

"Paprikás. Like my grandmother made. And arepas."

"Those don't go together."

"They do. That's the whole point of me."

She was quiet for a second, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped the way voices drop at the entrance to a hospital. "Dad. Do you even get to use that kitchen?"

I said of course, which was a lie shaped like a plan.

The kitchen I measure every other kitchen against was in Caracas, and it was confused in exactly the way I am confused. It made arepas and it made paprikás, sometimes in the same week, sometimes on the same stove at the same time — a German-Hungarian household running at full steam in the tropics, corn flour on one counter and a rolling pin nobody in that country sold anymore on the other. Nobody in that kitchen asked whether the combination made sense. The kitchen was the one room where being three things at once was not a problem to be managed. It was just what was for dinner.

Then I was thirteen, and in the United States, and I have spent the forty years since collecting addresses the way some people collect keys to doors that no longer exist. Venice. Los Altos. King of Prussia. Lake Worth. Somerville twice, if you count this room, and I'm arguing you shouldn't. I can tell you exactly what I cooked in each one, which turns out to be a shorter list than it should be, and the length of that list — not the zip codes, not the leases — is the actual record of where I lived versus where I stayed.

Thursday morning I came down to ask. Eileen owns the house. She is seventy-one, retired from thirty years of billing at Mass General, and she runs the place the way she presumably ran the billing: nothing owed, nothing floating, everything filed. She is not unkind. She salts the front steps before I'm awake. She leaves the sports section outside my door because I once said something about the Sox.

"Morning, Thomas," she said, which is not my name but is the name the house settled on early, and I stopped correcting.

I told her my daughter was coming Saturday. I asked if I could make a real dinner. Chicken, three hours, the oven and two burners. I'd clean everything and put it all back where it goes.

She looked at me for a moment, and then she looked at the counter, at a point on it I couldn't see anything special about.

"The kitchen isn't really set up for that," she said.

"I'd be careful. I know where everything lives."

"It's three hours of smell, a dinner like that. It gets into the curtains." She picked her crossword back up. "There's a place on Highland does family-style. You could take her there."

And that was the whole conversation. She didn't say no. People like Eileen never have to say no; they just describe the world, and the no is in the description.

I want something on the record, because of what came later. The labels were not in her handwriting. Hers is on the envelopes she leaves for the plow guy, round and forward-leaning; the tape on the shelves is block capitals pressed hard enough to emboss, and it slants left. I had noticed that in my first month. I'd decided Dennis was some earlier boarder with strong feelings about shelving, and stopped wondering, the way you stop seeing a crack in a windshield. I had also noticed that in fourteen months I never once saw Eileen cook anything that couldn't be completed inside a microwave, and that the kitchen was nonetheless kept like an instrument — boards oiled, knives whetted, the cast iron seasoned by somebody's hand. I took all of it as evidence of her regime. I noticed everything and understood none of it.

Friday night I had the teleporting dream again. In it I'm back in the Caracas kitchen and nobody is surprised to see me, and I know where everything is without looking. That's the entire dream. There's no plot. I wake from it the way other people wake from falling.

Saturday, Eileen left at ten for her sister's in Medford. I hadn't asked again. I'd decided to owe her an apology instead of a question, and I told myself a man of fifty-three is allowed one dinner on those terms. I own a spice collection that lives in my bedroom closet — if you want a one-sentence portrait of a man without a kitchen, there it is — and I carried it downstairs like contraband: the sweet paprika, the bag of P.A.N., the good salt. Chicken thighs and sour cream from the market on my own money, in my own bag, into a refrigerator with tape on the shelves.

By two the onions were down and going gold, and the house began to smell like my position on the world. For the arepas I needed a flat iron, and there is exactly one in that kitchen, so at four o'clock I lifted the skillet out of its spot for the first time in fourteen months. Under the handle there was tape I had never seen. DENNIS, block capitals, embossed.

I didn't hear the door, because of the exhaust fan. What I heard was nothing — the particular silence of somebody standing behind you.

Eileen was in the kitchen doorway with her coat still on. Her sister's hip is bad; the visits never survive lunch. She looked at the burners, at my bag of masa, at the steam on the window.

"I asked you not to."

"You said you'd rather I didn't." I turned the flame down slowly, like a man surrendering a weapon. "Lena comes at seven. I was going to apologize after."

Her eyes went along the counters, doing inventory, and stopped at the stove.

"Put that back," she said, and her voice did something I had never heard it do, which was fail.

"I need the flat iron for the arepas. I'll wash it and season it and put it back at the—"

"It goes back handle to the left." She crossed the kitchen and put her hand on the skillet's handle, next to the tape, and left it there. She wasn't lifting it. She was holding it down.

"I know the angle, Eileen."

"You don't know why." Behind us the onions ticked in the pot. "Dennis was left-handed."

I looked at the tape, and at her hand beside the tape, and the whole house quietly re-filed itself.

"My husband," she said. "Thirty-eight years. He cooked, I did dishes. That was the arrangement, and it was a good one." She took her hand off the pan and wiped it on her coat, which was not a gesture that meant anything about the pan being dirty. "The last winter he couldn't stand at the stove anymore, so he sat in that chair and pointed, and I made the labels while he told me where everything went. He said I'd never find a thing once he was gone." She looked at the shelf tape the way you look at handwriting. "He was right. I never look for anything. It's all where he said."

The first thing I felt was not sympathy. It was a very specific embarrassment: I had spent fourteen months being homeless at a woman who was more homeless than me. She had the deed and I had the lease, and neither of us could cook in that kitchen, and only one of us had a reason.

"Eileen—"

"You could have cooked," she said, and now she was angry, and it was better than the failing voice. "Any day. There's a label maker in the junk drawer. There's tape. Fourteen months and you never bought so much as a mug."

I started to answer and found I had nothing where the answer should be, and she watched me have nothing.

"You didn't want the kitchen," she said. "You wanted somebody to tell you no."

Behind me the onions went past gold and into brown, and past brown. Neither of us moved to save them.

The doorbell rang. Six-forty. Lena is early to everything; it's her mother in her.

Eileen straightened her coat. "Your daughter's here," she said, as if the bell were something I might not have understood, and went up the back stairs.

Some things you can rescue by starting over, and onions are one of them. It costs you twenty minutes, and your daughter sets the table in a stranger's kitchen, reading the tape on the shelves and saying nothing about it, God bless her. By eight the paprikás was on the table next to a plate of arepas, both of them steaming, the whole confusion of me on one table, and Lena ate like a person who had walked from New York.

"Okay, this is stupid good," she said. "Why have I never had this?"

"There was never a kitchen."

"There were like nine kitchens, Dad. I visited most of them." She tore an arepa in half and didn't look up, which is how she delivers her real material. "You keep looking at the door."

Eileen came down at nine, out of her coat, in the cardigan she wears for television. She stopped in the doorway of her own kitchen like a guest, and I watched her count the chairs. Lena, who has her mother's radar and none of her caution, was already up.

"Eileen, right? Sit — there's a mountain of it."

There are four chairs at that table. Eileen took the stool by the counter, and I understood which chair was which now, and put the understanding away where she couldn't see me holding it. She ate one plate in silence, and then, without being asked, a second, and something about the second plate hurt to watch in a way I still can't name past the word honored.

She said one thing the whole meal. She said it to her plate.

"The curtains are going to smell for a week."

"That's fine."

Sunday there was an envelope under my door. My name on it in her round, forward-leaning hand — I know the difference now. Inside, one line: *I'll have your deposit ready by the first. Nothing's wrong. — E.*

Nothing's wrong. I read it maybe thirty times, and I believe both sentences.

I didn't knock on her door to argue. I called Lena instead, and she was quiet, and then she said do it again at the next place, which is the most useful thing anyone has said to me in years.

On the first of the month I carried my boxes down — a man my age can still move himself in one carload if his life has stayed portable, which mine has, which is the confession hiding inside that sentence. Eileen stood on the porch with her arms crossed against the cold, and when I came back up for the last box she was holding the skillet out in front of her with both hands.

"Eileen, I can't take—"

"It doesn't stay here," she said, flat, the way she'd describe the world to the plow guy. "It doesn't stay here."

On the handle there was new tape, laid clean over the old. TOMÁS, it said. With the accent. Fourteen months of Thomas up the stairwell, and on the tape she'd put the accent where it goes, first try, pressed hard enough to emboss. At the edge, under my name, a corner of his tape still shows. I didn't peel it off. I'm not going to.

The new room is four blocks up Highland. The listing said kitchen privileges, which I have decided is the saddest two-word phrase in American English, and I signed anyway, because an address is an address and I have never once been without one. The skillet rode over on the passenger seat, label up, the only thing I own with my name on it spelled right.

Lena's taking the train up Saturday. I told her to come hungry. I haven't asked anyone's permission, and I'm not going to, and if the smell gets into the curtains, it gets into the curtains.
