# No Real Home (essay · partial)

I figured out what the depression was on a Tuesday, standing in a kitchen where the pans have labels.

Not my labels. I rent a room in a house that runs smoothly because everything in it belongs to somebody specific, and the somebody is never me. There is tape on the shelf that says a name that isn't mine, and the good skillet lives under the counter in a spot I know and don't use, because using it means washing it and drying it and putting it back at an angle I once got wrong. I stood there holding a jar of lentils I had no real way to cook, and the sentence arrived whole, the way true sentences do: I'm not sad. I don't have a home. Those are different problems, and I'd been treating the wrong one.

I have an address. I've always had an address — I want to be fair to my own life here. What I don't have is a kitchen, and I've started to think those are the two different words for home, and the address is the one that doesn't matter.

Here's my test, and you can run it on yourself: in the place you live, can you cook for someone else without asking anyone's permission? Not reheat. Cook — the kind that takes three hours and makes the whole house smell like your position on the world, and leaves a mess you're entitled to clean up slowly. If yes, you have a home. If no, you have a place where you sleep and a very personal relationship with takeout.

By that test I have had, in fifty-odd years across two continents, maybe three homes, and I only cooked my way through one of them.

The kitchen I measure every other kitchen against was in Caracas, and it was confused in exactly the way I am confused. It made arepas and it made paprikás, sometimes in the same week, sometimes — this is not an exaggeration — on the same stove at the same time, a German-Hungarian household running at full steam in the tropics, corn flour on one counter and a rolling pin nobody in that country sold anymore on the other. Nobody in that kitchen asked whether the combination made sense. The kitchen was the one room where being three things at once was not a problem to be managed. It was just what was for dinner.

Then I was thirteen, and in the United States, and I have spent the years since collecting addresses the way some people collect keys to doors that no longer exist. Venice. Somerville. Los Altos. King of Prussia. Lake Worth. I can tell you exactly what I cooked in each one, which turns out to be a shorter list than it should be, and the length of that list — not the zip codes, not the leases — is the actual record of where I lived versus where I stayed.
