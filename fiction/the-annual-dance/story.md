# The Annual Dance

Every December the Hernández family throws a party in Doral that is really a party from Caracas, teleported. Same songs, same aunts, same argument about whether the hallacas need more raisins, running continuously since before the country it comes from stopped being a place you could hold it in.

I have danced one merengue with Marisol Hernández at that party every year for thirty-eight years. One. Never the first song, never the last, no rule ever spoken out loud. Her husband knew, my wife knows, the aunts know, the DJ who took over from his father knows. It is three and a half minutes a year, which over thirty-eight years comes to just over two hours. I have spent more time than that waiting at the Palmetto and Bird Road. That's the whole affair. Two hours, spread thin as gold leaf over half a lifetime.

You want to know how it started, and the answer is disappointing: a wedding, 1987, we danced twice, and both of us understood at the same instant, somewhere in the second song, that a third would be a decision. So there was no third. She married Aurelio the following spring. I married Carmen, who I love, and I need you to hold both of those facts without dropping either one, because I've been doing it since the Reagan administration.

Aurelio died in March. Pancreas. He was a good man who sold industrial refrigeration and could quote Rubén Darío, and at his funeral Marisol held my wife's hand, not mine, which was correct, and the correctness of it sat in my chest for a week.

So then December. You see the problem. For thirty-eight years the dance was safe because it was impossible, and now the impossible part was buried out at Caballero Rivero under a very tasteful stone.

I thought about not going. Carmen watched me iron my guayabera and said, "If you don't go, everyone will know why. If you go, only everyone will know why," and I ironed the collar twice and we went.

The party was the party. The nephew DJ. The raisin argument, third generation. Rodrigo, Marisol's son, forty now, met me at the door with a handshake that lasted one second too long and a look I recognized, because it was the look of a man guarding a door he'd been guarding so long he'd forgotten there was ever a time before the door. He knows, I thought. He's known for years. Everyone at this party is a scholar of a three-minute subject.

Marisol wore gray, not black, and sat with the aunts, and was fine, fine the way a cracked windshield is fine, holding its shape because breaking would require a decision.

The merengue came at ten forty, the same Wilfrido Vargas the nephew's father used to play. Every year for thirty-eight years, at that opening horn line, I have stood up without deciding to, the way you stand for an anthem.

I stood up. The room did not look at us, which is how a room looks at you in Miami.

And Marisol shook her head.

Very small. Once. The aunts kept talking. The horn line went on being sixteen years old. She patted the folding chair next to her, and I crossed the room past the whole hallaca argument and sat down beside her, and we did not dance.

"If we dance now," she said, watching the floor fill up, "it's just dancing."

We sat there while the merengue we had rationed ourselves down to played itself out into a salsa, and she told me about the refrigeration warehouse, which she has to sell, and the paperwork of death, which nobody warns you is a full-time job for a year. I told her about my granddaughter, who is being scouted for volleyball at fourteen. Ordinary talk. The talk of two old friends at a loud party, which we had never once been.

That was the whole thing. That was this year's dance. Sitting down, four minutes, folding chairs.

Except when I got up to find Carmen, Marisol touched my sleeve, and she said, "Next year, though," and there was the whole country in it, the one we're both from, the one that doesn't exist anymore and never stopped existing.

I found Carmen by the door with her purse already on her shoulder, because she is always ready to leave a party ninety minutes before I am. She looked at me for a second, and then across the room at Marisol, gray in the folding chair, and my wife, who has known me since I was a boy with one good jacket, raised her glass of ponche crema about an inch. Not to me.

Marisol raised hers back the same inch.

I drove home on the Palmetto with all the windows shut, doing the only math I know how to do about any of this: thirty-eight dances behind us, and at our age maybe ten ahead, eleven if God is feeling generous. Thirty-five minutes, more or less, of a whole life. Next year, though.
