# Chapter 13 — The Letter

On Saturday morning, Niels returned Huygens's book to the university. When he came to the shop after noon, Anders's letter was twenty-one pages long.

The experiment began on page eighteen.

Niels stood at Karen's desk with his coat still on. He read the first page, turned it, read the second, and looked at the stack beneath Anders's hand.

"Is this the only copy?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"Because you have not sent it."

Anders took the pages back.

He had known the experiment came late. Each time he moved it forward, the account of Rømer and the old packet seemed to lose something necessary. Then he restored the missing pages and the experiment moved back again.

The first page named Huygens and listed the offices Anders knew he had held in Paris. The second described Bartholin's book. Then came the sailor, the first stone, Rømer on the stairs, Picard, Hven, the packet that had gone to France, and twelve years in which Rømer could have written.

There were four pages about the packet.

"He needs the history," Anders said.

"He knows Bartholin. He knows Rømer."

"He does not know what Rømer failed to tell him."

Margrethe was entering the week's sales at the other end of the desk. She had been quiet until then.

"Are you writing to Huygens or to Rømer?"

Anders turned in his chair. "Go upstairs."

"I am doing the sales."

"Do them tomorrow."

"Tomorrow is Sunday."

"I know what day it is."

She sanded the line she had finished and carried the loose sheet upstairs.

Anders heard her door close. He considered calling her back to finish the sales, although the sum was already written and he could have copied it himself. Niels was waiting, so he did not.

Niels removed his coat. He put it over the chair and sat down.

"She asked a fair question."

"You can translate it or not."

"I can translate the Latin. I cannot make him read it."

They worked for an hour. Niels translated the salutation and the first paragraph. He changed Anders's description of himself from maker of optical instruments to spectacle maker.

"I make other instruments."

"For other men."

"I made the apparatus."

"Then say that when we reach it."

They did not reach it. Niels had a burial before noon and left with one page finished. Anders worked alone until the church bells began. He put the translated page and the other twenty into Karen's desk, where they stayed.

There were months when Anders did not touch it. He repaired frames, filled orders, went to church, and did not open Karen's desk. Then he began again on cheaper paper and changed the order. Niels translated two more pages the following winter. Anders discarded one because it made his question sound smaller than it was.

In October of 1694, Anne Marie Rømer died. Anders went to the funeral and stood near Ole in the churchyard while the other mourners left. They spoke about the cold and the number of people who had come. Anders did not mention the letter. That night he wrote another beginning and put it in the drawer.

By the time Margrethe was sixteen, the drawer held nine unsent beginnings.

*

In February of 1695, Margrethe laid eight clean sheets of Dutch paper on the shop counter.

Snow had melted along the middle of the street and soaked the hem of her blue cloak. She kept the cloak on.

"A packet goes from Grandfather's counting room to Amsterdam on Friday," she said. "His man there will send a letter to Voorburg."

Anders set down the frame he was repairing.

"Hofwijck is at Voorburg."

"I know."

"How?"

"It is written in Huygens's letters."

"What letters?"

"The addresses Niels copied for you."

She took a folded commercial cover from inside her cloak. Mads's seal was already on it. The packet was thick enough for invoices and one private enclosure.

"Grandfather says you may put eight sheets in this. Not nine. The diagrams count. His clerks close it at noon tomorrow."

"Why did you ask him?"

"You have been asking how letters reach Holland."

Villum was sorting screws at the back bench. He had been sorting the same tray for most of the morning. He held one near his face, rejected it, then picked it up again.

"Ask what it costs," he said.

"I did," Margrethe said.

"And?"

"He said Anders could afford it."

Villum nodded. "That is not a number."

"No."

Anders touched the paper. It was heavier than anything left in Karen's desk.

"Friday gives us two days."

"I can help tonight. Tomorrow I am at Mads's."

"I will need a clean copy."

"Niels can copy."

"If he has time."

Anders looked at her cloak again. It was new. Not expensive, but new.

He had seen it at breakfast and assumed Mads had bought it for her. He had not asked.

"You are at Mads's today?"

"I was."

"On a Wednesday?"

"I go every day now."

Villum stopped sorting.

Anders said, "Since when?"

"Since last Monday."

"You did not tell me."

"I am telling you."

"For how long?"

"He offered me a place through Easter. If I do well, longer."

"What about the shop?"

Margrethe unfastened the wet cloak. "What about it?"

A customer opened the door before Anders answered. It was a cooper from Vesterbro who had sat on his spectacles. He maintained that he had placed them safely on a chair and that someone else had moved the chair under him.

Margrethe hung her cloak near the stove. She examined the bent bridge, named a price, and told him to return after supper. Anders watched her write the repair into the day sheet.

She did not ask whether the price was right. The cooper looked at Anders, but Margrethe waited without changing it. He agreed.

When the cooper had gone, Margrethe pushed the Dutch paper toward him.

"Eight sheets."

Anders went upstairs for the drafts.

*

They began with the shortest.

It was eleven pages. Anders had written it in 1692 after a winter sermon on the patience of Job. The first three pages described patience.

Margrethe read them standing by the counter.

"No."

"There is material here we can use."

"Where?"

She turned the first page over and continued.

Margrethe made three piles, though Anders had not agreed there would be piles. She read quickly and sometimes put a page down before reaching the bottom. Twice he moved a sheet back.

Villum found the correct screw at last. He fitted it into a frame, tightened it too far, and swore.

The second draft began with the third crystal. It did not explain what the first two did.

The third began with Huygens's own word, disposition, and argued with his book for six pages.

"Keep that one," Anders said.

Margrethe put it with the others.

"It has no instructions."

"The argument matters."

"Not if he cannot do it."

Anders pulled the pages back into a single stack.

"You have read half of one book about light."

"I have read nine starts of this letter."

"You were told not to."

"You left them in the sales drawer."

"That is not the sales drawer."

"It has held the sales since Mother died."

Villum left the back bench and went upstairs. He carried the screw tray with him.

Anders and Margrethe stood on opposite sides of the counter.

"What do you think should be in it?" he asked.

"What he must do. What he should see. What you are asking."

"That is not enough."

"Then he can ask for more."

"He does not know who I am."

"He will know who sent the letter."

"That is not the same."

"No."

She did not say anything else. Anders waited and got nothing from her.

He found the six lines he had written the night Rømer returned the packet. The paper had yellowed at one edge. Under Huygens's name, Anders had written his trade and crossed it out. The next beginning, about the first stone, had also been crossed out.

Margrethe read the lines.

"This one."

"It says nothing."

"There is less to remove."

They ate bread and cold herring at the workbench. Margrethe took off her sleeves and tied them over the back of a chair. Anders brought down the walnut case.

She had not touched it in front of him since the night she smeared one of Karen's observation tables.

He unlocked it and lifted out the holders, the pierced cards, the small brass circle, and the three pieces of spar. The iron on one holder had rusted at the screw. Margrethe fetched oil before he asked.

"If the letter is instructions," Anders said, "you follow them."

"Whose instructions?"

"Mine."

"The ones on the paper."

"Yes."

"You do not speak."

He almost refused. Then he took a blank sheet and began.

For the first few instructions, he kept his hands clasped behind him. That lasted until she chose the wrong card.

Place a candle behind a screen pierced with a small round hole.

Margrethe set the candle on the back table. She chose a screen.

"Which hole?"

"The small one."

There were three pierced cards in the case.

Anders crossed out small and measured the holes with dividers. He wrote the width beside the line.

Margrethe used the card he named. The candle threw a round spot onto a second card.

Set the first crystal in its holder.

She put the large piece in backward.

Anders made a sound.

"Is that on the paper?"

He shook his head.

The broad, sloping face had to turn toward the candle. He drew the piece from the side and marked the face in the drawing. Margrethe changed it. Two spots appeared on the card behind it.

Pass one ray and stop the other.

She held her hand over one spot.

"That stops it."

"You need the mask."

"You spoke."

Anders added a drawing of the first mask and the narrow opening that passed one branch onward. When she set it too close to the spar, the two spots had not separated enough and both struck the edge of the opening. He added the distance.

Margrethe had to clean soot from the card where she held it over the candle. She used the corner of a rag and left a gray mark across one thumb.

The cooper returned early. He stood in the doorway and asked why there was a candle burning in daylight.

"The bridge is not ready," Anders said.

"I came to watch."

"There is nothing to watch."

Margrethe carried the bent spectacles to the front window. She straightened the bridge while the cooper told her again about the chair. He held them to his nose, complained that the left lens sat low, and paid after she worked the bridge straighter.

Anders relit the candle. It had gone out while he argued about the price.

Margrethe returned to the apparatus.

The second crystal went into the larger turning ring. Anders's instruction said to set its principal section across that of the first.

"Show me a principal section," Margrethe said.

He pointed before remembering.

"On the paper."

He had used the term for twenty years. He had never needed to tell himself how to find it. They removed the second crystal and turned it over in their hands. The ink line from the old trials was still visible, but Huygens's pieces would not have that line.

Anders wrote a procedure for finding the two positions by turning the crystal and watching the chosen spot. It took half a page and did not work the first time Margrethe tried it. The holder covered a corner she needed to see.

They changed the holder.

By late afternoon the apparatus crossed most of the back table. Margrethe turned the second ring one mark at a time. At the final card, the chosen spot weakened. Other light remained above it and to the side.

"This mark?" she asked.

"Yes."

"It is not dark."

"Turn once more."

"The paper says stop at the mark."

Anders moved the ink line on the brass ring.

She turned it. The chosen spot disappeared.

The other spots did not. One lay near the edge of the card, and a weak one had fallen onto the table. The letter had to say which place went dark and which light they were ignoring.

Margrethe crouched until her eye was level with the card. She moved her head left and right.

"I can see it when I move."

"You are seeing around the mask."

"Huygens has a head."

They added a second screen.

When she could move without finding the spot, Anders gave her the third holder. He had written: place the third crystal between the first and second at an intermediate turn.

Margrethe put it midway between the two pieces.

"Intermediate turn," Anders said.

"You spoke again."

She turned the holder halfway around. Nothing appeared.

Anders took the brass circle from her, set it at the angle Karen had first marked, and stopped. He had done it without thinking.

Margrethe looked at his hand.

He returned the circle to its previous position. Then he drew the orientation. The drawing was poor. He started another.

"Mother drew the holders better," Margrethe said.

"Your mother drew everything better."

"Her circles were bad."

"They were."

Margrethe found Karen's old diagram in the notebook. The lower corner still held the ink smear. She did not look at Anders when she opened to it.

The last time they had sat over that page, Anders had struck through Margrethe's figures and told her to look what she had done. He had not opened the book with her since.

She copied the holder onto a loose sheet. Her lines were narrower than Karen's and less certain at the curve. She added the first stone, then the second, and showed the third between them.

After that, she reset the apparatus without help.

The chosen spot went dark. She inserted the third crystal at the copied angle. A dim spot returned at the mark.

She removed the third crystal. The spot went away.

She put it back. The spot returned.

"The candle is lower now," she said.

"It has burned down."

"Then the distances on the paper have changed."

Anders replaced the candle.

They did it again.

He had been waiting for her to say something about the returned spot. She checked the candle distance instead. On the second trial, she found the light more quickly than he did.

*

Niels arrived after dark with a boy from his parish waiting outside. The boy had been told to fetch him if an old woman near Saint Peter's worsened.

"She has been worsening since Tuesday," Niels said. "Today she decided it was urgent because I came here."

The boy accepted a bowl of stew from Villum and sat on the shop step.

Niels read the new instructions. He made Anders replace every use of this crystal with first, second, or middle.

Margrethe copied the diagrams onto two of the Dutch sheets. Niels translated the method into Latin on three more.

The method and drawings used five sheets. The other sections still had to fit on three.

Anders had brought down a passage about Karen. It said she had recognized the importance of the work when no one else did and had never doubted what they saw.

Margrethe read it twice.

"She doubted the brightness."

"That is in the table."

"You wrote never."

"She did not doubt the experiment."

"She said the second spot was weaker than you said. She said it for years."

"That was measurement."

"Then write that."

Anders took the page from her.

"I am trying to tell him who made the observations."

"Her name is not here."

He looked down. He had written my late wife throughout.

There were six uses of the phrase. Anders had wanted Huygens to understand that another person had done the work with him. Karen's name did not appear once.

Niels moved his chair closer to the lamp but did not speak.

Anders crossed out the first line. He wrote Karen's name. He wrote that she had devised the reference comparison and kept the brightness table with him. He did not write that she had never doubted.

Margrethe brought the notebook.

The table had been made across many winters. Candle distances changed. One set used the old aperture. Two rows had no second observer. Karen had marked those rows herself. Anders had always intended to repeat them.

"We cannot send all of this," Margrethe said.

"It is the record."

"It is three records."

"Then send three."

"On what paper?"

They copied the cleanest trials. Where the observers had disagreed, Margrethe put both tallies. She labeled them estimates.

The boy on the step came in to say the old woman was worse.

Niels stood.

"I will come back before morning."

"You will be tired," Villum said.

"I am already tired."

He took his coat and left with the boy.

Villum went upstairs soon after. He carried the stew pot and forgot the spoon.

Anders and Margrethe worked alone.

The section about Rømer was nine pages. Anders reduced it to four and could go no farther.

Margrethe read the first paragraph aloud.

"In the year 1672, I entrusted an account of this observation to Ole Rømer, now professor of astronomy at Copenhagen, to be communicated in Paris."

"That stays," Anders said.

She read the next paragraph. It described Huygens's 1678 presentation, Rømer's presence, and his failure to write.

"Does Huygens need this to repeat it?"

"He needs to know why it arrives now."

"The date tells him."

"The date tells him I am late."

"You are late."

Anders walked to the stove. The fire was low. He put on another piece of wood and waited for it to catch.

"He will think I read his book and added a stone."

"You have the old pages."

"Rømer has seen them."

"Then write the year and keep the old pages."

"That asks him to believe me."

"You are writing him a letter."

He looked at her.

"What do you think this is?"

"Instructions."

She had said it quietly. She began cutting the second paragraph.

Anders let her.

The final paragraph gave the 1672 date and Rømer's name. It did not say what Rømer had failed to do.

Near midnight, Margrethe put down the knife.

"I have to sleep."

"We have one page left."

"You have one page left."

"The copy must go tomorrow."

"Then wake me when Niels comes."

"I need you to check the drawings."

"I checked them."

"Once."

Margrethe looked toward the stairs.

"Grandfather does not pay me when I am not there."

"He knows you are helping me."

"That will not close his accounts."

She waited.

Anders asked, "What does he pay?"

She told him.

It was a proper wage. Less than a male clerk received, but not a child's allowance.

Anders looked toward the cash drawer. He knew what was in it without counting.

At that amount, the shop could have paid her for two days in a good week. It could not match Mads's offer.

"For copying?"

"For finding what his clerk copied twice."

"You could do that here."

"I do."

Margrethe gathered the unused paper and took it upstairs.

*

Niels returned before sunrise.

The old woman had recovered enough to criticize the psalm he chose. He took this as a good sign.

They finished the Latin while Margrethe slept. The last page contained three questions. Had Huygens observed a return at the selected mark when a third piece was inserted? Could his account of the disposition explain why the middle crystal restored that path? Would he or someone he trusted repeat the arrangement?

Margrethe had cut the paragraph explaining Huygens's disposition the day before. They put back three sentences from it. Anders read them aloud once and left them alone.

Anders signed it:

*Anders Pedersen, spectacle maker of Copenhagen.*

He stared at the line. Then he added that the procedure had been followed from the written account by his daughter Margrethe, with no instruction beyond what was on the page.

He had meant to sign only his own name. The added sentence took four lines, and he had to recopy the bottom half of the sheet to make room.

Niels read the addition.

"That is useful," he said.

"I know."

"I meant to him."

They woke Margrethe. She checked the diagrams, corrected a copied distance, and read the Danish version against Niels's Latin. She did not object to the final line.

At ten, they sealed the letter inside Mads's commercial cover.

*

The counting room smelled of wet wool and orange peel.

Three clerks were closing accounts for the Amsterdam packet. Mads sat at the high desk with his sleeves covered in black cloth. He opened the private enclosure, counted the sheets, and held one diagram toward the window.

"This is the glass business?"

"Yes," Anders said.

"Not a petition?"

"No."

"Then it can go with the accounts."

Margrethe told him he had counted the cover as one of the eight sheets.

Mads counted again and got seven.

He closed the packet and pressed his seal into fresh wax. One of the clerks put it between an invoice for dyed wool and a complaint about two missing barrels of herring.

Anders watched the clerk tie the bundle.

"The man in Amsterdam knows where to send it?"

"He will if he wants my next order," Mads said.

Margrethe was already at the second desk. A column of figures waited beside her, along with a heel of bread and half a dried orange.

"Are you coming?" Anders asked.

"At six."

He had forgotten.

On Monday morning, she left for the counting room before the shop opened. She wore the blue cloak and carried her lunch under one arm.

Villum asked whether she had taken the good bread.

Anders looked in the cupboard.

"She did."

"All of it?"

"Half."

Villum found the shop key by touch and handed it to him. Anders opened the shutters.
