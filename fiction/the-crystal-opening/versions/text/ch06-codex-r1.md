# Chapter 6 — Paris Takes Him

For three months, Rømer missed more Thursdays than he kept.

Jean Picard had arrived from France with clocks, telescopes, a quadrant, and a list of work. When Rømer came to the shop, he brought part of the list. A clock case would not close in damp weather. A screw on the quadrant needed replacing. Picard wanted two small lenses ground to a French curve before Saturday.

He had come to determine the position of Tycho Brahe's old observatory on Hven. Paris wanted to compare Tycho's observations with new ones. To do that, Picard needed the distance between the vanished observatory and Paris more exactly than any map gave it.

"They know the island is there," Anders said.

"Not well enough."

"It has not moved."

"No. That helps."

Picard had brought two clocks, a quadrant with a telescope fixed to it, a sector, and long tubes that took two men to move safely. He inspected each instrument after the voyage. Rømer inspected them after Picard. Then he brought the damaged pieces to Klosterstræde and asked Anders to inspect what they had both missed.

Villum liked the French payments and complained about the French deadlines.

"Saturday," he said after Rømer left another request, "is the day after tomorrow in Denmark."

Still, he took the orders. The payments arrived under Bartholin's seal and were entered in the ledger without delay. For the first time since Anders had bought the second crystal, the box beneath the bench held more than it had before.

One morning in October, Rømer came before the shop opened. His coat was buttoned wrong.

"The cross is gone from the smaller telescope," he said.

"What cross?"

"At the eye. Two fine lines. You place the star where they meet."

"Gone how?"

"One line parted. Picard says it can be repaired on the island. The man repairing it says he has repaired it. The line now moves when the tube is turned."

"Then he has not repaired it."

"You see why I came."

Rømer wanted Anders on Hven that day. A boat carrying supplies would leave at noon. The next observation of Jupiter's innermost moon was expected after midnight, and Picard wanted the smaller telescope ready before dark.

Villum listened from the workbench.

"What does it pay?" he asked.

"The Academy pays Picard. Picard pays for what he needs."

"That sounds slow."

"Two marks," Rømer said. "One now."

Villum looked at Anders.

There was a fitting at four for a councilman's daughter. Anders had ground the lenses. Her headaches had defeated two other spectacle makers, and Villum had said the fitting must be done by the hand that made them. A satisfied councilman would bring better work than instruments sent through three men.

"We can fit her tomorrow," Anders said.

"She leaves tomorrow."

"You can fit her."

"I can."

Anders waited.

Villum took the mark from Rømer and put it on the counter. "The boat returns at dawn?"

"If the wind holds."

"He must be here by noon."

"He will."

"I asked him."

Anders said, "I will."

The crossing took longer than Rømer had promised and made Anders sick over the side twice. Hven sat low in the water ahead of them. Farms and church land rose green from the shore. Nothing in the view announced that the most famous observatory in Europe had once stood there.

Rømer waited until Anders could stand before mentioning the sickness.

"Tycho kept a tame elk," he said as they climbed from the landing.

"Why?"

"A nobleman needs something to fall down his stairs."

"Did it?"

"It drank beer first."

Anders stopped on the path. "That cannot be true."

"I thought you would like that part."

"I do. I do not believe it."

"Those are separate pleasures."

Uraniborg was less than a ruin. Local people had taken much of the stone. Grass covered the rest. Picard's men had opened parts of the ground to find the old foundations, and the lines of the former house showed as trenches and patches of broken masonry. A wooden shelter stood near the center with a slit in its roof. Instruments crowded the shelter and the ground around it. Clock cases sat on planks above the damp. A telescope tube longer than the shop lay under oiled cloth.

Anders had expected more stone and fewer people carrying boards.

Jean Picard wore a priest's black coat under a heavier traveling coat. He was not old, but he had the narrow, dried look of a man who forgot meals without suffering from it. He took Anders's hand when Rømer introduced him and turned it palm upward.

"The grinder," he said in Latin.

Anders answered in the same language. "Spectacles, Herr Abbé."

Picard's eyes moved to him properly for the first time. "The grinder has Latin."

"Enough for glass," Anders said.

Rømer hid a smile.

The damaged sight was housed in a brass ring at the eye end of the telescope. One of the two fine threads had broken. Its replacement had been fixed with soft wax that changed shape near a lamp and pulled the crossing away from the center. The ring itself had also bent at one screw.

Anders asked for a candle, a needle, hard wax, a scrap of black silk, and two narrow slips of wood. Picard gave the list to a man without asking what any of it was for.

"Can it be done by dark?" Picard asked.

"If the ring does not break when I straighten it."

"Will it?"

"I will know after."

Picard looked at Rømer. "He answers like an instrument maker."

Anders thought of correcting him, then looked at the telescope, the clocks, and the roof built to open around them. There were worse things to be mistaken for.

The ring did not break. He warmed it in his hands and pressed it flat between the wooden slips a little at a time. The silk was too thick. He untwisted one strand and chose a single fiber, then fixed it across the opening with hard wax. The work was smaller than anything Villum permitted after midday, when the shop light grew poor. Here Picard had two lamps brought close and a boy holding a white card beneath Anders's hands.

When the crossing held, Rømer aimed the tube at a stake on the far side of the trench and turned the instrument through its positions. The crossing stayed on the same cut in the wood.

Picard tried it. He called the repair *exact*.

He said the word to Rømer.

Rømer translated it though Anders had understood.

They worked until sunset. Picard wanted the clocks compared, then compared again. He wanted the telescope's line checked against the quadrant. He wanted the wooden pier tested after each man stepped away from it.

Anders knew this kind of work. Picard checked people as often as he checked instruments.

After dark, Jupiter appeared above the opened roof.

Picard let Anders look through the repaired sight before the observations began. The planet was a bright disk. Near it sat small points that Anders first took for ordinary stars. Rømer named them quietly. Io was the one they would watch.

"It goes into Jupiter's shadow," Rømer said. "Here, we note when. In Paris, Cassini notes when. The difference gives the distance between us in time."

"You measure land with a moon."

"We measure where the island is."

"The island is under us."

"You remain difficult to impress."

Anders moved from the telescope. "No. I am trying to understand what the measurement says."

Rømer's expression changed. He took a page from the table and drew the two places, the planet, and the moving moon. He explained it again without the language he used around Picard. Paris and Hven watched the same event. Their clocks disagreed because noon did not arrive together. The shared eclipse joined the two clocks.

"The shadow is the signal," Anders said.

"Yes."

"And the light tells you when it happened."

"Yes."

"If the light required time to come here—"

"Then both places would receive it late."

"The same amount?"

Rømer looked toward the telescope. Picard was calling for him.

"Near enough," he said. "Hold that question."

He went back to the instrument.

The eclipse came later than Anders expected. Nothing crossed in front of anything. One of the small points merely weakened and vanished. Rømer called the instant. A man at the clock called a number. Picard repeated it. Rømer wrote.

Then they waited.

Cold entered through the open roof. Anders stood outside the shelter and looked at the dark foundations. Tycho had built a house here for measuring everything. Kings had visited it. Emperors had sent gifts. Now a French priest had crossed the sea and dug trenches to learn exactly where the house had been.

Rømer came out carrying two cups of heated beer.

"He asked me to remain," he said.

"Tonight?"

"For the work. Through spring."

Anders took the cup. "Bartholin agreed?"

"Bartholin recommended me."

"Then you knew."

"I knew Picard had asked."

"When?"

"September."

Anders looked at the bag Rømer had brought from Copenhagen.

"How many shirts are in there?"

"Two."

"You came to repair a telescope."

"A man should have a second shirt."

"On a one-day trip."

"It has been useful."

Anders drank. The beer was too hot and tasted of iron from the pot.

"You did not tell me."

"I am telling you."

"After I came here."

"I wanted you to see the work."

"You wanted me to understand why you said yes."

"That too."

"You had already said yes."

Rømer looked back through the shelter door. Picard stood over the clock while another man read the quadrant.

"Not aloud," he said.

The wind changed before dawn. The supply boat did not leave. By noon the water between Hven and the Danish coast was white and steep. Anders stood at the landing with his tool roll while a fisherman explained that a councilman's daughter could be the king's daughter and he still would not take a small boat into that water.

They returned the following afternoon.

Villum had fitted the spectacles. The councilman's daughter had rejected them. Whether the lenses were wrong, the frames hurt, or she had already decided to reject what came from the shop, Villum did not say. He had returned the deposit.

Anders put Picard's second mark on the counter.

Villum did not pick it up. "Did the instrument work?"

"Yes."

"Then one of us had a satisfactory customer."

He went into the back room.

Rømer remained on Hven through the winter. When ice stopped the regular boats, he worked in Copenhagen with Picard's calculations and left again when passage opened. The Thursday table gathered dust. Anders used it for orders.

He earned back the four skillings. He earned the guild fee. Villum accepted the coins but did not arrange the examination.

In April, Rømer returned to the shop carrying a French clock screw wrapped in paper. He had also bought new boots.

"Your father?" Anders asked.

"Picard."

"He bought you boots?"

"He paid me."

Rømer put one foot on the bench rail so Anders could inspect the leather. Then he announced that Paris had agreed to take him.

He would leave with Picard in May. There would be work at the new observatory. Cassini and Huygens attended the Academy. The pay was not much, but Rømer would no longer need money from his father.

Anders closed the shop for midday and bought beer. Villum did not object. The three of them drank at the bench among the trays of lenses.

"To Paris," Villum said.

"To returning from it," Rømer answered.

Villum approved of that and poured again.

After he went upstairs, Anders brought out a folded packet. It contained twelve pages. He had drawn the apparatus in separate parts so it could be rebuilt without him: pierced card, first crystal, mask, intermediate crystal, second mask, final crystal, marked window. The turning tables were copied cleanly. At the end he had written what they knew and, in another column, what they did not.

Rømer read every page.

"Your figures have improved," he said.

"My figures were always good."

"Your letters, then."

"Take it to Huygens."

Rømer did not answer immediately.

"You will be in the same Academy," Anders said.

"So will a hundred men who want him to read something."

"This is twelve pages."

"That will comfort him."

"Show him."

"I will try."

"That is not a promise."

Rømer folded the packet along its old crease. "I promise I will put it in front of someone who can answer it."

"Huygens."

"If I can reach him."

"You are going to his Academy."

"And on my first day they may ask me to wind clocks."

Anders took the packet back.

Rømer held on to the other end.

"I said I would try."

Anders let go.

"Write to me," he said.

"Every month."

"You will not."

"Every two."

"You will write when something happens."

"That I can promise."

Rømer put the packet inside his coat.

At the door, he took Anders by the shoulders and kissed his cheek. It was how learned men and merchants parted after wine. They had never done it.

"Do not lose the third piece," Rømer said.

"You lose more things than I do."

"I find them eventually."

"You never found the glove."

"I know where it is. It is on Hven."

"That is not finding it."

Rømer left with Picard in May.

The first letter came from Hamburg. The second came from Amsterdam and described a clock that kept better time on a ship than any clock had a right to keep. The third came six months later on paper headed with the mark of the Royal Academy.

It contained three pages about Paris, two figures of a new micrometer, and no mention of the crystals.
