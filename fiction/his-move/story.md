# His Move

The first postcard came three weeks after we buried my father. On the front, a photograph of the cathedral at Pécs. On the back, in fountain pen, in a hand like barbed wire pulled straight: "Nf3. Your move, old friend. — B."

My father and Bela Brack had been playing chess by mail since 1984. I knew this the way you know your father has a pancreas — a fact somewhere in him, doing whatever it did. A move a month, sometimes two, Hungary to Pennsylvania and back, through the end of one country and the shrinking of another, through my mother's whole illness, through everything. Forty years. I did the arithmetic once: they had played, in all that time, eleven games. They were, when he died, thirty-one moves into the twelfth.

I wrote back on a proper card, the kind you buy for exactly this. I said my father had passed in February, peacefully, that I was grateful Mr. Brack's friendship had meant so much to him, that I was sorry to be ending their long game this way.

His reply took eleven days. "I know he died. His sister wrote me, she has better manners than you. Nf3 stands. It is his move; I have known his hands for forty years. We are perhaps twenty moves from the end of something we began before you had your driver's license. You will help me finish it. Look in his desk — he kept the games. Bxc5 would also not surprise me, but he would regret it by winter. — B."

I handled it reasonably at first. I called Aunt Zsofi. She said, "Oh, Bela," in the tone you'd use for a stubborn roof, and then, less helpfully, "Finish the game, szívem, what does it cost you." I said I didn't play chess. She said my father hadn't either, at first, and hung up on me, at eighty-six, like a teenager.

The desk had the games. Eleven manila folders, plus a twelfth, open, the position taped inside the flap in my father's engineering-neat notation, every move of thirty-one annotated in his hand: not just the moves — the man. "B. plays the bishop early when Marta is unwell." "B. offers the trade here as an apology for October." "Held the knight back. He hates when I do this. Doing it."

I did what you'd do. I found a chess app, entered the position, and let the engine think. It thought for two seconds and said the best move was a rook to d1, evaluation plus one point four. I copied it onto a postcard of the Philadelphia skyline and mailed it to Pécs, feeling efficient, feeling, if I'm honest, clever — my dead father backed by a machine that had digested every game ever played.

Brack's answer came back so fast it must have crossed my card over the Atlantic.

"That is not your father."

Nothing else. No move. The game just stopped, the way a heart does, and it turned out I minded, a lot, in a way I could not have predicted and did not enjoy. I'd been sending a dead man's moves for one month and I already understood Bela Brack completely: it was not chess. It had never been chess. It was the last channel on which my father was still transmitting, and I had put a machine on the line, and the old man had heard the substitution inside one move. One. My mother couldn't always tell my father's handwriting from his brother's. Bela Brack could tell his soul from a supercomputer through six thousand kilometers of postal system, off a single rook.

So I learned my father. That is the only way I can say it. Eleven folders, forty years, every annotation. I learned that he never took a pawn Brack left as bait before move twenty, out of respect and suspicion, which in him were the same organ. I learned he attacked when he was sad — the games from my mother's two bad years are all fire, both men burning the board down rather than say anything. I learned that in 1997 the notation stops for five months and resumes with no explanation and a queen sacrifice, and I have a guess now about 1997 that I never had while he was alive.

I set the position up on his actual board, on the actual desk, and I sat there some evenings with his reading lamp on, hand hovering, asking a question that has no grammar: not "what is the best move," but "what would you do, you specifically, you who held the knight back because it drove your friend crazy." And I moved the knight. Not the engine's rook. The knight, held back for six moves already, doing the thing Brack hated, because the annotation said "doing it" and the man who wrote that was still, somewhere in the notation, doing it.

I mailed it and heard nothing for three weeks and slept badly, like a man waiting on a biopsy.

The reply was a longer card than usual. "There he is. Kg7. Tell me — the knight. Did you find it in his notes, or did it make you angry to play it? Both are him. — B. P.S. He is going to win this game. He was always going to win this game. I have known for a year and he knew too and we went on, because the winning is not the point, God help the two of us if the winning was ever the point."

We finished in March. Twenty-two more moves, five months, me playing slower and slower, and I won't pretend some of the moves weren't beyond what the folders could teach me — twice I chose blind, on nerve, on the accumulated grammar of a man I was only now, at forty-four, meeting. My father mated him with the held-back knight and I sat at the desk and cried like a fool over a chess position, which is the only time I have properly cried about any of it.

The last postcard is framed above the board. "He wins, 7–5 overall, and I claim the moral games. Your last ten moves: eight were him, two were you. The two were not bad. Come to Pécs while I am alive, which is not a long window. I will show you the games where I knew him better than his family did, and you will tell me about 1997, and we will see which of us knows more. Bring the folders. Your move now, in the larger sense. — Bela."

I fly out in October. I've started a new folder, thirteenth, empty. First game I ever play with my own name on it, and I already know he's going to slaughter me, and I already know what he'll say while he does it, because my father wrote it down in 1989: "B. is kindest when winning. It is his one flaw as an opponent and his best quality as a friend."
