# The Reasonable One

Our mediator has a machine that reads your marriage. She didn't call it that. She called it "communication pattern analysis," in an office in King of Prussia between a dermatologist and a tax preparer, and she slid a laminated page across the table like a menu.

"Both parties export their complete message history," she said. "The service builds a neutral communication profile. Couples find it clarifying."

Four years I'd been told I was the cold one, the manager, the man who ran the marriage like a standup meeting. And here was a referee. Not her sister, not her therapist, not the couples counselor who nodded at everything Dana said. A machine. Machines count.

"Yes," I said, fast enough that the mediator blinked.

"Sure," Dana said, faster, and I took that as a bluff, because I was ahead on points and had been all morning.

It took eleven minutes to export four years of marriage. Forty-one thousand two hundred and three messages, mine and hers together, uploaded to a company in Austin with a soothing blue logo. The confirmation screen said my data would be treated as confidential. Nobody warns you about the other part, which is that it will be treated as data.

Two weeks later we sat in the same chairs and the mediator had the report in a folder, forty pages, and the look of a woman who has read ahead.

"I can summarize aloud," she said, "or you can each read privately."

"Read his first," Dana said.

The mediator found the page. "Party A initiated seventy-one percent of all exchanges."

I nodded. Seventy-one percent. Four years of being the one who reaches out, in writing, notarized by Texas.

"Party A's most frequent opening phrase: 'Did you get a chance to.'"

Dana laughed, once, through her nose. I didn't see what was funny. It's a polite phrase. It has *chance* in it, it assumes the best about people.

"Ninety-four percent of Party A's questions concerned tasks or scheduling," the mediator read. "The remainder concerned arrival times."

"Arrival times are scheduling," I said. "It's double-counting."

"He's disputing the math," Dana said to the mediator, to the folder, to Austin. "Note the timestamp."

The mediator kept her eyes on the page, and something in her posture told me we were arriving somewhere she'd already been. "Party A communicates care primarily through logistics," she read. "Party B stopped requesting care in year two."

The room went quiet in a specific way, the way a kitchen goes quiet when the refrigerator you've never once heard stops humming.

Year two. We were still good in year two. That was the year of the lake house, the year her father got sick and I handled everything — the flights, the specialists, the insurance appeals. I handled everything. I was already hearing which word in that sentence was doing the work, and I opened my mouth to ask whether the model accounted for context, and closed it, because I heard my question before I said it, and it was a task.

Dana leaned forward and put her hand flat on the folder, first thing she'd done in an hour that wasn't aimed at me.

"Now read mine."
