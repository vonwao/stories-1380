# Florida Man, Corrected

Voice memo one. Testing. This is Ray Alvarez, recorded on my own phone in my own kitchen, with the refrigerator making that noise Linda says is normal and I say is a compressor begging for help.

Callie, you asked me to tell the stories in order, all five, and stop correcting strangers in comment sections, because strangers in comment sections are not, quote, a jury of my peers. You wouldn't say why you wanted them. From you that means the reason is either very good or a surprise party, and I've decided not to push my luck by asking twice.

The first headline is from 2018.

FLORIDA MAN STEALS TWELVE-FOOT ALLIGATOR FROM MINI GOLF COURSE.

Correction. It was fiberglass.

That matters. A live alligator has rights, or at least teeth. This was a fiberglass alligator named Captain Chompers from Treasure Lagoon Mini Golf, and I did not steal him in the spiritual sense. I moved him. Dale, who owns Treasure Lagoon, owed me one hundred and eighty dollars for pressure washing the pirate ship, the cave, and the blue whale, which is a lot of mildew shaped like family entertainment. When I came for the check, Dale told me the work was incomplete because I hadn't washed the alligator, and he told me this without looking up from his phone. He was scrolling the whole time. I want that in the record, and you'll see why by memo five, or you won't, and that's fine too.

Small claims court exists for this. I watched a video about it. The video said eight to twelve weeks. So yes, after two beers, I loaded Captain Chompers into my truck.

This was wrong. I understood it then, but with less legal vocabulary. The deputy who responded was named Reyes, first time I met him, and he stood in my driveway looking at a twelve-foot fiberglass alligator and said, "Sir, walk me through your thinking," which remains the fairest question anyone has ever asked me.

The photo makes it look worse because the alligator's head is sticking out over the tailgate and I'm giving a thumbs-up. I'd like the record to show the thumbs-up was for a man in a Jeep who yelled, "Hell yeah," which is not an argument but does feel like one at the time.

Nobody was protected in this story. There is no deeper wound. I was forty-six years old and mad about a fake alligator. I want one memo where I'm just an idiot, so you know I know the difference.

Voice memo two.

FLORIDA MAN DRIVES GOLF CART ON INTERSTATE WITH GOAT IN PASSENGER SEAT.

Correction. It was the shoulder of a state road, which is still bad but not nationally bad. And the goat wasn't in the passenger seat. The goat stood where the golf bags go, because he'd eaten part of the seat the week before.

The goat's name is Lionel. Lionel belonged to a kid named Matty who raised him for 4-H and then found out, late in the process, what the H's were leading to. Nobody had explained it to him straight. Adults do this to children constantly — we say livestock and project because we're afraid of saying, you have been brushing the animal we plan to sell by the pound. Matty asked his mother. His mother was at work. He asked the 4-H coordinator, who gave him a pamphlet. A pamphlet, Callie. So he called me, crying, because I'm the kind of adult children call when the plan is already bad. I accept that about myself. It's not a compliment.

My truck was at Linda's because the starter was doing performance art. My neighbor's golf cart was in the driveway with the key in it, because this county is held together by trust and bad habits. I put Lionel in the back and made it eleven minutes before a sheriff's deputy lit me up, and when I saw it was Reyes I felt something I can only call relief with a badge on it.

I told him the truth. He said, "Sir, that is not better." But he called the farm himself, the one west of town with the two donkeys, because it turns out Reyes knows every place in the county that takes in animals whose paperwork has gone emotional.

The headline says I was wearing a bathrobe. True. It was Matty's emergency, not mine, and emergencies don't wait for pants.

Lionel lived six more years at that farm with a woman who called every animal baby. Matty's in college now. He pretends not to remember, because he's nineteen and has a girlfriend, and I respect that by not using his last name, even though everyone back there knows exactly which goat I mean.

Voice memo three.

FLORIDA MAN THROWS CHICKEN TENDERS AT CITY COMMISSIONER.

Correction. One tender. Singular. Also, it missed.

This was the public meeting about the marina leases. I had three minutes to speak, same as everyone, and I'd written my comments on the back of a Chicken Kitchen receipt because that was the paper in my truck. The commissioner — he has enough Google already, no name — looked at his phone the entire time I talked. Not glanced. Looked. Thumb moving. You may be noticing something by now. I noticed it slower, and standing up.

I said, "Are you hearing any of this?"

He said, "Your time is still running."

That is when the tender left my hand.

For Callie, and for any future employer who finds this audio after I die and thinks, this man had impulse control issues: correct. But the tender didn't leave my hand for me. In the second row was Mrs. Alvarez, no relation, eighty-three, whose husband kept a skiff at that marina for thirty-one years and died in it with the radio on, which is probably the exit he'd have picked if anyone got to pick. She'd spent the morning practicing her comments in English because she didn't want the translator. She got up there, said her three sentences, and the commissioner looked at his phone. There is a certain kind of looking-away that is a whole system wearing one man's face. I didn't have those words then. I had a tender.

Not proud. Not sorry in the clean way people want. Sorry for the throwing. Not sorry someone looked up.

Voice memo four.

FLORIDA MAN BREAKS INTO RETIREMENT HOME TO SING JOURNEY.

Almost completely false, except for the parts that are stupid.

Assisted living, not a retirement home. And I didn't break in — the back door by the laundry had been broken since Hurricane Irma, which I know because I fixed it twice for them, and both times the facility replaced my good hinge with a cheap one, because the good hinge wasn't in the budget and the cheap one was. Remember that door. It's the only door in these five stories that anyone official ever touched, and they touched it wrong twice.

I did sing Journey. "Don't Stop Believin'." I am not proud of the key. It is too high for me and for most men with honest throats.

This was for Linda's aunt Mercy. Real dementia, not the movie kind — mean some days, gone some days, back for twenty seconds in the middle of lunch and furious about the soup. She'd been a waitress at the old Jai-Alai fronton and could carry six coffee cups in one hand until arthritis took the trick away. The facility locked down visitors after a flu outbreak, which was reasonable, I'm not anti-rule. But every afternoon at four, Mercy asked where Linda was, and every afternoon a kind, busy, underpaid person told her Linda would come tomorrow. True in the administrative sense. False in the part of a person that keeps score.

Linda asked me to drop off a blanket. I dropped off the blanket. Then Mercy saw me through the laundry door and said, "Raymundo, sing the one from the bar."

Nobody calls me Raymundo except Mercy, and my mother when I've made a priest necessary.

So I sang. Quietly, then less quietly, because Mercy said, "Don't sing like you're stealing it." Three residents came into the hall. One nurse filmed it, which is how the internet got involved. Security arrived during the second chorus, which is a merciful place to stop that song.

The headline made me a lunatic, fine, I've worn worse shirts. But Mercy's daughter called Linda from across the state, yelling about dignity. Mercy had dignity. She also had the flu, dementia, and one song she could still ask for by insult. Sometimes dignity is a word used by people who have never been lonely at four in the afternoon.

Voice memo five.

FLORIDA MAN SETS OWN BOAT ON FIRE DURING HURRICANE WARNING.

This is the one you told me to leave alone, Callie. I considered it. I made coffee. I fed the neighbor's cat, which is not my cat no matter what Linda says. I sat here looking at the phone for twenty minutes. But if the other four are going in the record, this one is the record.

The boat was a seventeen-foot Mako with a soft transom and a motor that started when it felt emotionally supported. For six months I'd told anyone with ears I was going to fix it. The hurricane was supposed to turn north. They always say that until they don't.

The boat was on its trailer in my driveway when a woman I will call S. knocked on my door with two trash bags, a backpack, and her little boy holding a pillowcase full of dinosaurs. Plastic ones, for the pedantic listener.

S. cut my hair for eight years at the shop by the Winn-Dixie. She once told me my beard made me look like a divorced magician, which was unfair, because I was only separated at the time. Her husband had rules — about the car, the phone, the bank card, the weather, her mother, the boy's shoes. Men like that are very interested in rules. It makes them feel less like criminals. She had called the number you're supposed to call, once, months before. There was a report. There was a case number. The case number did not drive her to her sister's before the bridges closed, and her husband had the car that night, and mine was the last house on her street with the lights on.

We were loading my truck when the husband's brother drove by slow.

Here is the part where people want bravery. I wasn't brave. I was scared and angry and already halfway into a bad idea. The brother knew the boat — everybody knew the boat. So I rolled it into the street, poured gas in the bilge, and lit a rag with the grill lighter.

This is not recommended hurricane preparation.

The brother stopped for the fire. So did half the block. Fire has that old authority — it is the one thing left that makes everybody look at the same place at the same time. While my boat became a lesson, S. and the boy got into my truck through the passenger side, and we left by the alley behind the duplexes, and I drove them west in weather that made every overpass feel personal.

Reyes took my statement two days later. I told him I'd been siphoning fuel and made a mistake. He looked at me a long time — he knew the boat, he knew the street, I believe he knew the case number — and he wrote down my stupid lie in careful handwriting, because it was the kind of stupid lie that protects the person it's meant to protect, and then he charged me with careless burning, which I had certainly done. The internet got the mugshot. My hair was bad because of the storm, not because of character.

S. is all right. The boy is in middle school and apparently very tall. I know because every Christmas a card comes with no return address, a picture of him next to something he's outgrown. Last year it was a bicycle. This year it was his mother.

I'm not saying I'm a good man. Good men do not solve problems by committing arson against their own recreational equipment. I'm saying the headline is too small. They always are. A headline is a door with no room behind it, and people love doors they never have to walk through.

Voice memo six.

No headline for this one. Yet.

Callie, you came by Sunday and told me why you wanted these. You're not fixing my reputation. You said the baby is going to google his grandfather someday, and the internet will hand him five doors with no rooms behind them, and you wanted him to have the rooms.

I've been sitting here since, thinking about what's actually in them. An unpaid invoice. A pamphlet. A phone in a man's thumb. A cheap hinge. A case number that couldn't drive. That's the collection, kid. Every story where they say your abuelo went crazy, look for the part where somebody official was supposed to show up, and didn't, and check who they sent instead. It was a fat man in a bathrobe more times than anyone planned.

Hold on. Somebody's at the door. It's ten forty at night, which means it isn't good news, and the porch light shows me Dolores from the corner holding her phone like it's burning her, so whatever this is, it has already started.

Leave the recorder running, Callie. If there's a headline tomorrow, this is the part they never print.
