# The Smallest Restaurant in California

The complaint described me as an unpermitted food facility. I have six chairs.

The facility is the backyard of a house I rent in Los Altos, and the six chairs came off Craigslist from a dentist's waiting room. When I got laid off in March I told myself I'd keep one fixed point in the week while everything else was in the air, and the fixed point turned out to be Thursday dinner. I cooked, and whoever came, came. By June the six chairs were full every week and there was a bowl by the gate. Nobody had to put anything in the bowl. Retirees put in folded twenties. The grad student two doors down once put in an IOU and paid it off in tomatoes, which is the kind of economy I could believe in for the rest of my life.

The inspector came on a Thursday, naturally, at seven, when the whole yard smelled like my grandmother's kitchen in Caracas. His name was Dave, Santa Clara County Environmental Health, and he had the face of a man who'd been sent to shut down a lemonade stand and knew it.

"Are you selling food?" he asked.

"There's a bowl."

"Is the bowl required?"

"The bowl has accepted tomatoes."

Dave wrote something down. At the table, six forks had stopped in the air. He said there'd been a complaint — anonymous, he couldn't say more, he genuinely seemed sorry about all of it — and then he looked past me at the pot on the outdoor burner.

"What is that?"

"Hallacas. My grandmother's. They take two days and apparently they're illegal in this state."

"They smell incredible," he said, miserably.

I asked him what it would take to be legal. Not to argue — I actually wanted to know. I had nothing but time, and I'm the kind of person who reads the rules the way other people read box scores. Dave recited it like a man who'd had to say it too many times: a commercial kitchen, which a rental backyard is not. A three-compartment sink. A ventilation hood. A grease trap. A food manager's certificate, that one's a class and an exam. And a dedicated handwashing sink for employees only.

"I don't have employees."

"Then nobody can use it," Dave said. "But it has to be there."

I looked at the sink that had to exist so that no one could use it, in the kitchen I wasn't allowed to have, to serve the neighbors who were at that moment pretending to study their napkins, and I felt something click that hadn't clicked since March.

"Okay," I said. "Then I'll be a restaurant. The smallest one in California."

"That's not —" Dave looked at the table, six people holding forks, nobody chewing. "That's not usually how people take this."

I walked him out through the gate, past the bowl, and that's when the other thing started ticking, the one I still haven't put down. It's a short street. Eleven houses. Somebody on it filed the complaint, and every house on that street has had somebody at my table at least once.

Except one.
