# Two Breakfasts

The Sea Grape Motel has twenty-two rooms, a pool the color of weak tea, and me at the desk from ten at night to six in the morning, which after nineteen years is less a shift than a citizenship.

Here is what Lourdes told me when she trained me, back when the carpets were a different bad color: on October the fourteenth, a man checks into room 9. He pays cash. He signs the book "R. Ellis," which may or may not be his name, in handwriting from a time when they graded you on it. You do not put him in any other room. At six in the morning you carry two breakfasts to the door — coffee and eggs on one tray, tea and toast, dry, on the other — and you knock twice and you leave it and you go back to the desk. By seven he is gone, the key is on the pillow, and the money for both breakfasts is under the key, plus a tip that is always a little too much.

"Who's the second breakfast for?" I asked, because I was new.

"For whoever it's for," Lourdes said, and that was the whole training on the subject.

Nineteen Octobers, this arrangement has held. Mr. Ellis got older the way a lighthouse gets older, which is to say barely, and correctly. We never spoke past the weather. Tea, dry toast, the fourteenth. Some years I forgot until I saw the date on the deposit slip and felt the little bell go off in my chest: him.

This year the Sea Grape got bought. A group out of Charlotte, eleven properties, an app. The new manager is Bryce, who is twenty-nine and not a bad person, and that's what makes it hard; villains you can just hate. Bryce has systems. Card on file, government ID scanned into a tablet, no cash check-ins, corporate policy, no exceptions, it's actually about protecting YOU, Dee.

I explained about October the fourteenth. I explained badly, because there's no way to explain it well; every version has a hole where the reason should be. Bryce listened with his customer-service face and said the man was welcome to stay like anyone else: card, ID, tablet. "He'll adapt," Bryce said. "People adapt."

The fourteenth was a Tuesday. Mr. Ellis came through the door at eleven forty, in the same tan windbreaker, older this year in a way that scared me, folded at the corners like the bills he was already taking out.

I looked at the cash. I looked at the tablet, asleep on its little stand with Bryce's policy inside it.

"Card and ID now, Mr. Ellis," I heard myself say. "New owners." And I watched something in his face start to close, quietly, like a tide going out — not anger, worse, a man rehearsed at doors shutting — and before it closed all the way I put my own card in the machine and ran the room on it, and typed my own name where the guest goes. "There," I said. "You're me tonight. Pay me the cash, that part's between citizens."

He counted it out, and then he stood there a second, and he said the most words he's said to me in nineteen years: "She doesn't like the new light out front. Too blue." And went to room 9.

I sat with that at the desk all night, in the blue light, which is, in fact, new, an LED thing Bryce is proud of, and I want to tell you the night felt strange, but the truth is it felt like every October fourteenth: quiet, ordinary, and slightly at attention, like a room where someone's sleeping.

At six I carried the trays. Coffee and eggs. Tea and dry toast. Knocked twice, left it, didn't linger, because in nineteen years I have never once lingered, and I wasn't going to insult anybody by starting.

At seven, room 9: key on the pillow. Money under the key, both breakfasts plus a tip that covered, to the dollar, what a night at the Sea Grape cost before Charlotte bought us — I checked against the old rate card, don't ask me why I still have it. The coffee tray, eaten. The tea tray, untouched, toast cold, everything exactly as I'd set it.

Except both cups had been turned. Handle to the left. I set handles to the right; nineteen years at a desk, your hands have policies too.

I stripped the room myself. In the drawer of the nightstand, where the Bible is, there was also, this year only, a hairpin. Old style, real tortoiseshell. It could have been there for a decade, is the reasonable thing to say. Housekeeping isn't what it was.

I ran the deposit at the end of my shift. Cash goes in an envelope with a slip, and old habit, I copied the register line for the room. And then I did something I'd never had a reason to do: I went into the storage closet where the paper registers from before my time are boxed, and I found October, and I will only tell you what I found because you've listened this far: R. Ellis, room 9, appears every October fourteenth back as far as the boxes go — and in the oldest book, 1981, the handwriting signs in for two. Mr. and Mrs. The second name is a little rushed, a little laughing, the way you sign when someone's pulling your sleeve toward the room.

After 1981, one name. Two breakfasts.

Bryce found the charge in the system, of course. My card, my name, occupancy mismatch. He gave me a warning in writing, which he was required to do, and while I signed it he kept not quite looking at me, and finally he said, "The eggs and the toast thing. My grandmother did something like that. Plate at Thanksgiving." He filed the warning in his tablet where policies live. Then he said, "If he comes back next year, text me first. I'll figure out the system part."

That's all. That's the story. It's October, the porch light is still too blue, and I've decided the hairpin stays in the drawer with the Bible, because it isn't mine, and because if there's one thing a night clerk learns, it's that you don't clear a room somebody's coming back to.
