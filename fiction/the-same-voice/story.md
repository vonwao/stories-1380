# The Same Voice

My brother made me promise two things when he asked me to be his best man: that I would not mention Tampa, and that I would write the toast myself. "Yourself yourself," he said. "I want it bad and real, not good and fake. Swear on Mom."

I swore on Mom. Then I went home and opened a chat window, because I am a coward with a laptop, which is the most dangerous kind.

Look, I'd tried. I sat with a legal pad for two nights like it was 1974. Everything I wrote was either an inside joke that required footnotes or the truth, and the truth involved Tampa. So I typed "warm funny wedding toast, brother of the groom, we grew up sharing a room, his wife made him gentler" and the machine gave me something with a shape. Three beats about the kid he was, a pivot — "but here's the thing nobody tells you about my brother" — a callback at the end, exactly one tasteful crack in the voice. I rehearsed it until it sounded like me. I told myself the ideas were mine and the machine had only done the mortar, which is what everyone tells themselves, which is how you can tell it's a lie.

The wedding was in Coral Gables, at a club with ceiling fans the size of helicopters. And the thing I had not planned for was that the bride's father toasted first.

Hector is a serious man, a retired judge, the kind of father-in-law you address like a traffic stop. He stood up with one printed page, and he began, "There are three things I've learned about love," and my stomach dropped through the ballroom floor and kept going until it hit the aquifer.

Three things. Then the pivot: "But here's what nobody tells you about marriage." Then, I swear before God and this assembled company, a callback to the three things, and exactly one crack in the voice, positioned like a throw pillow.

It was my toast. Not the words — the skeleton. The same skeleton wearing a judge.

I looked around the room. Two hundred people, moved. His wife dabbing her eyes. And me, sitting there with my own printed page in my jacket like a fake passport, watching a seventy-one-year-old man deliver my ghost's other client.

Hector sat down to real applause. And as he sat, he folded his page in half, and over the top of it, across two tables of centerpieces, he looked directly at me. And I understood that he had heard nothing wrong in his own toast, but he had seen me hear it — seen the exact face of a man listening to his own skeleton. His eyes did not say sorry. His eyes said: so it's a family plan now.

Then the DJ said, "And now, the best man."

I stood up. I took out my page. My brother was watching me with his whole open face, the face from the top bunk, the face that had said swear on Mom.

I put the page back in my jacket.

"So," I said, with nothing in my hands, "Tampa."

My brother's face did several things at once that I will treasure until I die.

I told Tampa. All of it: the borrowed boat, the wrong keys, the manatee that was not, in the crucial moment, a manatee, my brother standing in four feet of warm seawater at 2 a.m. holding a churro he had defended through the entire disaster like it was regimental colors. I told it badly. I lost the order, doubled back, said "wait, I skipped the part with the churro" out loud into a microphone at a country club. No pivot. No callback. There was a crack in my voice but it was load-bearing, not decorative.

And the room did that thing rooms do for the badly told true story and never quite do for the other kind: they leaned in instead of back.

At the bar afterward, Hector found me. He had two whiskeys and gave me one, which from a retired judge is a knighthood.

"You went off script," he said.

"I did."

"I noticed the script." He took a slow sip. "In my defense, I hate speaking in public more than I hated criminals."

"In my defense," I said, "I swore on my mother, so I'm going to hell either way."

He laughed, and we stood there, two frauds at an open bar, and it should have ended there, it was a good ending. But then my brother came over, sweaty from dancing, and put an arm around each of us, and said he wanted to thank us both, because the toasts were perfect, because you could tell — and here my soul left my body through the ceiling fans — you could tell they were real.

Hector and I said nothing with tremendous discipline.

"Mine took me eleven drafts," my brother said, misty, holding us both. "The vows. Eleven. Yours came out so natural."

I caught Hector's eye. Neither of us will ever ask him. Some things you leave in four feet of warm seawater, holding your churro, waiting for morning.
