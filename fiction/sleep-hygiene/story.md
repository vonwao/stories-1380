# Sleep Hygiene (opening)

The patient in Bed Two was a retired plumber from Medford named Doug Passanisi, and at 3:11 in the morning he began describing, in Spanish he does not speak, the bakery on the corner of the street where I grew up.

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is my whole profession. I have spent nineteen years attaching electrodes to strangers and watching them do the one thing I can't, and in nineteen years I have learned that sleeping people say all kinds of things. They confess. They apologize. They laugh at jokes nobody told. One woman recited a full Wendy's order with terrible urgency, sat up, said "no pickles," and slept until morning. Sleep-talk is noise from a brain doing maintenance. It is not information. At 3:10 that night I would have signed my name to that sentence.

Doug was in for a routine apnea study because his wife had reached the limit of what a person can forgive acoustically. He went down fast, which I always notice and always envy, and for four hours he was a textbook: the slow waves rolling through like weather, the snore, the little gasps his wife had described with her hands in the intake meeting. An ordinary man having an ordinary night. I was at the console with my second coffee, watching four sleeping strangers breathe.

At 3:11 the audio monitor picked up his voice, and his voice said, "La panadería de la señora Yépez."

Nobody knows that name. I don't mean it's obscure. I mean it exists in exactly two places: a street in Caracas that was demolished and rebuilt into something else while I was in the eighth grade in a different hemisphere, and me.

He kept going. The blue awning, faded on the side where the sun came over the hill. The hill itself, steep enough that the delivery boys stood on their brake pedals with both feet. The smell of rain arriving on hot concrete, which is not a Massachusetts smell, which is not a smell this man from Medford has ever had any business knowing.

I did what a technician does. I checked the leads. I checked the gain on the mic. I pulled up his intake form: languages spoken, English. On the EEG Doug was in stage two sleep, unremarkable, the spindles coming at decent intervals. His mouth kept moving.

Here is the part I have never told anyone, because there was never a way to start the sentence. The street Doug was describing was not the street as it was. Señora Yépez died when I was eleven; the awning came down before that. What Doug was describing was the street the way it appears in my dream — the version where it is always ten minutes to six in the morning, where the bakery is always about to open, where the light behind the counter is always just coming on. I have had that dream, in the ten-minute scraps that are all the sleep I get, nearly every night for twenty years. It is the most reliable thing in my life. I have never said one word about it to another human being.

"Ya viene la luz," Doug said, comfortable, like a man reading a menu. "She's turning on the light. She's always about to open."

And then, in Spanish, in his sleep, in a Medford accent: "He's here again. The one who keeps the street. He looks so tired."

I checked the leads a third time. I want you to understand that about me. A man was narrating the inside of my skull through a lapel mic, and my working theory was equipment.

Around five Doug went quiet and slept like a payroll of angels. At 5:40 the acquisition software closed the study and did something I had never seen it do: it flagged the file and generated a referral. Not for apnea. The code wasn't in our manual, and the referral wasn't addressed to any pulmonologist I knew — it went to something called the Regional Office for Sleep Continuity, which sounds like nothing, which sounds like a department that schedules other departments. I tried to cancel it. There is no way to cancel it. I know exactly how thoroughly there is no way, because I looked for one until the day staff arrived.

Doug woke at six, cheerful, remembering nothing. He asked if he'd said anything embarrassing.

"You ordered a sandwich," I said.

It was the first lie of my professional life, and it came out of me smooth as a vital sign.

I ate breakfast where I always eat breakfast after a shift, a diner on the square that has been open twenty-four hours a day since before the square had a name. I have gone there for eleven years, and for eleven years the old woman in the corner booth has been part of the furniture. Hungarian, somebody once told me. The waitresses top off her coffee without being asked and guard her booth like it's load-bearing. She talks, when she talks, about streets nobody can find on any map, and everyone is kind to her the way you are kind to the harmless mad. Once I watched her tell a cop that the neighborhood "held" because of her, and the cop bought her pancakes.

That morning she was asleep sitting up, hands folded, straight-backed as a churchgoer, and I remember thinking: ten minutes. She sleeps the way I sleep.

When I passed her booth on the way to the register, her eyes opened.

"Go home and rest, Tomás," she said. "Somebody's covering your street until Thursday."
