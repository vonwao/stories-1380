# The Astronaut's Son

Marcus Deel moved to our school in February, which is the worst month to be new, and by lunch on his first day he had told four kids that his father was an astronaut.

This was outside Philadelphia in 1994, in one of those townships that is mostly parking lots for a mall, and we were eleven, which matters. Eleven is the last year a kid can say "my dad is an astronaut" and have the room genuinely split. Ten, everyone believes you. Twelve, nobody does. Eleven is the hinge, and Marcus had arrived at the hinge in a coat with a broken zipper, claiming the sky.

I believed him for nine days. Nine days of asking him about g-forces at lunch, and him answering, carefully, like a man handling government property. He was good. He said his dad was "between missions." He said the reason there was nothing about him in the library's space books was that his dad did "the classified kind, mostly weather." He said they moved here to be near his grandmother, and that part had the boring grain of truth to it, and a good lie rests on a boring truth the way a ladder rests on a wall.

Then on a Saturday my mom dragged me to the mall, to the Sears side, and at the key-cutting kiosk in the middle of the concourse there was a woman in a red vest, and standing next to her, handing her keys off a little hook, was Marcus. And I looked at him and he looked at me, and his mother said, "Who's this, sweetheart?" in a voice that had never in its life been to Cape Canaveral, and Marcus said, "Nobody. Kid from school," and turned around to face the machine that grinds keys.

I didn't say anything on Monday. That's the part people don't understand about being eleven: I never even considered saying anything. What I considered, walking home, was orbital mechanics — how a thing that heavy stays up. And I decided the answer was speed, that a lie stays up the same way a satellite does, by moving fast enough that it keeps missing the ground, and that Marcus was going to need help with velocity.

So I helped. God help me, I became the mission's ground crew. I checked out "Rockets, Missiles, and Space Travel" and fed him details. When Danny Kubiak, who was born a prosecutor, started asking why Marcus's dad never came to anything, I invented the training rotation in Houston: six weeks down, two weeks home. I drew the schedule on graph paper. Danny examined it like a customs form and let it through, that time.

The end came in April, and it was the school's fault, not ours: they announced Career Day. Parents, in person, at a folding table, with your last name on a tent card.

Danny Kubiak got to Marcus by the buses, with an audience. "So he'll come, right? Your dad. Career Day. Astronaut's basically the number one career." And there it was, the ground, coming up at nine hundred miles an hour, and Marcus, hinge-age Marcus, in the broken-zipper coat, looked at all of them and said his dad would come if the rotation allowed, and looked at me.

And I said — loudly, easily, the way I would testify to it for years afterward in the courtroom of my own bed — "My uncle works at the Cape. Marcus's dad did the weather package on the shuttle before last. I've seen the mission patch."

I had never lied like that. Not the size of it, the fluency. It came out with landing gear. Danny actually backed up half a step, and for one entire day, the lie flew again, and what I felt — it took me thirty years to name it — was joy. We had built something together, and it worked, and holding up the sky with another kid is the closest thing to friendship's pure form I have ever touched.

Career Day, Mrs. Deel came. Red vest. She sat at the folding table with a ring of blank keys and a hand-lettered card, KEYS & LOCKS, and she showed a bored crowd of sixth graders how a key is a shape of a secret, how the grooves are a password made of metal, and she was, objectively, the third or fourth best presenter there. Marcus's dad did not come, because, as everyone by then had quietly assembled without any announcement — the way schools know things, through the walls — there was no astronaut, there was a man in Allentown that nobody named twice.

Nobody said anything to Marcus. Danny didn't even gloat, which at eleven is a species of mercy.

Here's what I don't tell people, when I tell this story at the age I am now. Marcus never spoke to me again. Not a fight, nothing to point to. He simply moved his lunch spot, changed bus seats, managed the hallways so we never met, with the same care he'd spent on the classified weather missions. It took me until embarrassingly recently to understand it, and I understood it all at once, in the shower, at forty: the lie could survive being disbelieved. Danny disbelieving it was weather, part of flight. What it could not survive was a witness on the inside — someone who had seen the kiosk AND fed the ladder AND knew, all along, exactly how high we were. Every kid at that school had watched his sky fall down. I was the only one who'd helped him hold it up, which meant I was the only one who'd been up there with him when it went.

You can forgive the crowd. The crowd is physics. The crew is personal.

His mother cut keys at that kiosk for years. I know because I checked, every time my mom took me to Sears, from a distance, the way you check on a grave. And once, in high school, I finally needed an actual key cut, and I stood in line, and Mrs. Deel took my house key off my ring and clamped it in the machine, and she looked at me for a second with a face I couldn't read, and said, "You were his friend," past tense, no question mark, and handed me back two shapes of the same secret, and charged me for one.
