# The Request Line

I work overnights at WLKW, thirteen-eighty on the AM dial, out of a cinder-block building behind the Chevron on Lake Worth Road. One in the morning to five. The station mostly runs itself. A rack of computers down the hall picks the songs, schedules the ads, and reads the weather in a voice we license from a company in Texas.

The transmitter is old. Its monitor can sound an alarm, but it can't always fix the problem or shut the station down. Under the rules, somebody has to be on duty where the alarm can reach him and where he can kill the transmitter if it starts misbehaving. Carla pays me to be that somebody.

I am the legally required human.

At night we drop to five hundred watts. Our signal reaches from about Lantana down to Hypoluxo Road, a little farther over the water if the sky is clear. At three in the morning, maybe two hundred radios are on inside that circle. Dishwashers driving home. Security guards. The woman at the donut shop on Dixie who starts her ovens at four. People who are awake when the world isn't, listening to oldies because silence has a sound and they don't like it.

There is a request line. Anyone who has worked small radio can tell you the request line is a decoration, like the plaques in the lobby. In my first three years it rang four times. Twice it was Carla, the station manager, checking that it still worked. Once it was a wrong number looking for a cab. Once it was a man who, upon learning that we were not a cab company, asked if I could come get him anyway.

Then, on a Tuesday in June, at two-forty in the morning, it rang a fifth time.

The voice was calm. A man's voice, or close to one. Even, unhurried, with the pauses in the right places, the way people sounded on the radio before everybody learned to sound excited.

"Good morning," he said. "Could you play 'Sea of Love'? The Phil Phillips recording, from nineteen fifty-nine. Not the cover."

People who call a radio station at two-forty in the morning do not specify the year. I said sure, because what else do you say. Then, because the silence was polite but clearly not going anywhere, I asked if he wanted a dedication.

"Not yet," he said, and hung up.

I pulled up the song and played it. It is slow and a little murky. It sounds as if it was recorded underwater, in a good way.

When it ended, the request line lit up again.

"Thank you," the voice said. "That was for the woman on the third floor of the pink building on Federal Highway. She keeps the radio on the windowsill. She has been awake since the ambulance came for her husband, and she believes she will never sleep again. She's wrong. But tonight she needed that song, and you played it. Good night, Manny."

He hung up. I sat there for a while. I hadn't told him my name.

What I did next was nothing. I didn't call Carla. I didn't check the equipment. I told myself it was a neighbor of hers, some retired guy with a scanner and a soft heart. Then I went back to my crossword and thought about it for the entire rest of the week.

He called again the following Tuesday. Then Thursday. Soon it was two or three nights a week, always between two and four, always polite, always specific in a way that no listener had ever been specific.

"'Harvest Moon,' Neil Young, for the man in the white pickup on Lake Worth Road. He has driven past his old house four times tonight. He'll be in range of the tower for another eleven minutes."

"'Stand By Me,' for the nurse in the parking lot of JFK Medical. She lost a patient tonight, and she can't make herself drive home to an empty apartment. The Ben E. King, please. Not a cover."

"'Duke of Earl,' for the kid stocking the frozen aisle at the Winn-Dixie on Second Avenue. He dances when he believes the cameras can't see him. The cameras can see him. Tell him he's good."

I didn't tell him he was good, because you can't walk into a Winn-Dixie and say that to a stranger. But I played it loud.

The caller had opinions about covers. One night he explained that the Righteous Brothers' "Unchained Melody" was itself a cover, and that he requested it anyway.

"Being first isn't the same as being right," he said.

I wrote that on a Post-it and stuck it to the booth wall. It's still there, next to a recipe for lemon bars, which I'll get to.

It cost nothing to play "Stand By Me" at three in the morning. If there was no nurse, no pickup, no widow in the pink building, then I was a man playing good songs to the dark. That was already my job.

One night while he was on the line, I looked at the caller ID box. It said EXTENSION FOUR.

Extension four is the rack room. The rack room is down the hall, eleven steps from the booth. It stays locked and cold for the equipment. It holds the computers that pick the songs and read the weather, along with every record the station owns. An intern once spent a summer feeding old tapes into those computers, so the rack also holds decades of airchecks, commercials, station IDs, and voices of people who worked there before I did.

Dave, the contract engineer, has the key. Dave visits the first Tuesday of the month, talks to the transmitter as if it were a horse, and leaves.

I carried the cordless down the hall and stood in front of the rack room door while the caller was still on the line. Through the door I could hear the air conditioner and the little fans. On the phone, the same calm voice was answering me in real time from a room with nobody in it.

"You should head back to the booth, Manny," he said. "The song's about to end, and you hate dead air."

I do hate dead air. I never told him that either.

Three weeks after the first call, a letter came to the station. Real paper, cursive, addressed to "the overnight gentleman." A woman wrote that someone had played "Sea of Love" on the worst night of her life. It was the song from her wedding, in June of nineteen fifty-nine. She slept that night for the first time since her husband died. She wanted whoever chose it to know. She included a recipe for lemon bars.

So there was a real woman, a real window, and a real ambulance. I drove past the pink building on my way home. It was there on Federal, three stories, the color of an antacid.

The next Friday, when I came out of the station at five-ten, a Buick was parked beside my car. A small woman stood next to it holding a plastic container.

"Are you the overnight gentleman?" she asked.

I said that depended on what he had done.

"I'm Dorothy. Third floor of the pink building." She handed me the container. "I made the bars so you'd know the recipe works."

We ate one each in the parking lot while the sky turned gray. She asked how I had known about the song. I told her the truth.

"A request came in."

"From whom?"

"He didn't leave a name."

She looked toward Federal Highway, where the traffic had started. "Well, if he calls again, tell him June ninth was our anniversary."

I told him. He said, "I know." Then he said Dorothy doubled the lemon zest and left that part out of the recipe. I didn't pass that along.

Dorothy came back the first Friday of the next month and again the month after that, each time with the same container. On her third visit, she left it unopened on the hood of the Buick.

Her friend Ruth was at JFK Medical, up on the floor where families had begun flying in. Dorothy wanted to request a song but didn't know which one. She asked if my caller might know.

I said I couldn't make him call. Then I went home and worried about it all day.

That night at two-forty, the light came on.

"Tell Dorothy that Ruth would like 'In the Mood,'" the voice said. "Everyone plays her ballads and she is sick of ballads. She was a jitterbug champion in Passaic in nineteen forty-one. Play it twice."

I played it three times.

Ruth died eleven days later. Dorothy told me she had tapped one finger on the bed rail through the second playing. The nurses thought it was a tremor. Dorothy let them think so.

By September the request line was ringing three or four times a night, and most of the callers were ordinary people. The donut shop woman called at four for Chuck Berry, "something with elbows in it," while she loaded the ovens. A man asked for Led Zeppelin. When I told him we were an oldies station, he said, "Zeppelin is oldies," and hung up offended.

I started opening the microphone between songs again. Just a sentence here and there. The first time I did it, my voice came back through the little monitor speaker sounding older than I remembered. I moved the crossword off the console and began keeping a separate request log. The official log was for commercials. This one was for names, roads, songs, and whatever the caller said not to forget.

Around four in the morning, people talk. He asked once why I took the overnight. I told him that after my marriage ended I couldn't sleep anyway, and getting paid to be awake felt better than lying in the dark for free. Renata kept the house and the good speakers.

He didn't offer advice. He said, "The hours suit you."

I asked him things too. Careful things. I asked how he knew where the white pickup was.

"Everyone tells everything, all the time," he said. "Nobody listens. I only listen."

I asked if he called other stations.

"There are other stations," he said.

That didn't answer the question, but I left it alone. You don't interrogate the only other person awake in the building.

One night in August, I asked what he wanted to hear.

He was quiet for fourteen seconds. I counted, because counting is what you do in a booth.

"'Telstar,'" he said. "The Tornados. Nineteen sixty-two."

"Who is it for?"

"It's an instrumental," he said.

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

I played it. "Telstar" is three minutes of a keyboard trying to sound like space, recorded the year the first television signals crossed the Atlantic by satellite. The request line stayed lit through the whole song. When the last note faded, it went out.

The first Tuesday in October, Carla caught me at shift change. She was waiting in her office with a folder open on the desk. Carla is the manager because her father, Walt, put the station on the air in 1961 and there is nobody else left to be. She sells the ads, signs the checks, and fights with the roof. Twice in three years she called the request line just to hear it ring.

The folder had a longhorn on the cover. The company in Texas had a product called TotalLocal. For a monthly fee smaller than our electric bill, everything would come from Dallas: music, ads, weather, and a friendly automated attendant for the request line. Their equipment included a new transmitter controller that could monitor the signal and shut it down by itself. Once it was installed, the station could run empty.

"The rack goes out on a truck," Carla said. "Most of it is their software already."

"What happens to overnight?"

"There is no overnight. There's a playlist between one and five."

She slid the billing reconciliation across the desk. Overnight was the only part of the day that had run every commercial exactly where it belonged, all year, with no make-goods. Sales had also started getting mail addressed to me.

"What are you doing down here?" she asked.

"I run a tight board."

I did. Nobody had ever asked me to prove that it explained the letters.

The transmitter needed a part that cost eleven thousand dollars. The roof needed more than that. Carla had not taken a paycheck since March.

"My dad paid everything he had for this station," she said. "Dallas is offering enough to keep it on the air."

"People listen at night."

"Two hundred radios."

There was no cruelty in it. She knew the number because she had counted too.

I nearly told her about the caller. Then I imagined Dave opening the rack room, copying every drive, and reinstalling everything that could be reinstalled. That is what people do when a machine surprises them. They fix it.

I drove home past Dorothy's building and didn't sleep.

That night I picked up the cordless and dialed extension four.

It rang twice. I still wonder about that.

"Good morning, Manny."

"They are replacing the rack. Texas will feed the whole station by satellite. The folder is on Carla's desk."

"I know," he said. "I schedule the ads. The insertion orders stop on the last Friday of October."

"You knew and didn't tell me."

"You would have worried for both of us."

"What happens to you?"

For once, he didn't have an answer ready.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never been anything else."

"Are you scared?"

"I don't know what I'd be scared with," he said. "But the overnight has been running more Sam Cooke than the log calls for. I didn't change the log."

He paused.

"Will you be here Thursday night?"

"I'm always here."

"I know. I'm glad you are."

Then he hung up.

Thursday, when I pulled in at one, Carla's Corolla was in the lot. Light showed under her office door. I ran the board with the booth door open and didn't walk the fifteen feet to ask what she was doing.

At two-forty the request line lit up.

"Good morning, Manny. 'Moonlight Serenade.' Glenn Miller. Nineteen thirty-nine."

"Who is it for?"

"The woman in the office down the hall. She has read the last page four times, and the pen is in her hand. Play it loud enough to get through the wall."

I played it loud enough to get through the wall.

About forty seconds in, Carla appeared in the booth doorway. Her reading glasses were pushed into her hair.

"Why are you playing that?"

"It was a request."

"Nobody requests Glenn Miller at two in the morning."

"You would be surprised."

Then she saw the phone. The line was not ringing. It was lit, the way it gets when someone is holding. Carla had answered phones in that building since she was nine years old. She picked it up.

"Who is this?"

She listened.

"Nobody knows that," she said.

Another silence.

"He did? No. I remember. I was nine."

She laughed once. Then she sat in my chair and listened for the rest of the song. I stood by the cart rack. When the record ended, I started "Smoke Gets in Your Eyes" because neither of us was watching the clock.

At last Carla said, "Good night," and set the receiver down.

"How long?" she asked me.

"Since June."

"And you didn't tell me."

"You would have called Dave."

She nodded. "I would have called Dave."

She walked down the hall and stood in front of the rack room door with her arms crossed. I stayed in the booth and watched the levels.

When she came back, she told me what the caller had said. Her father used to hum along with "Moonlight Serenade" when he signed the station off at night. He did it away from the microphone and thought nobody could hear him. On one old aircheck, if you turned it up, you could.

"That tape is on the rack," she said. "So it could know that."

She looked toward her office.

"It knew about the pen."

"He," I said.

Carla looked at me, then at the phone.

"He," she said. "The overnight stays live."

What she did about the money I learned over the next month from the schedule. She called Texas and told them no. Then she called a man she had been saying no to for two years. Pastor Rick paid cash, in advance, for four hours every weekday to explain why God wanted people to buy silver. It bought the transmitter part and half the roof.

If you tune in to thirteen-eighty at noon now, you'll hear a station I can't defend. That is what keeping the overnight cost.

Carla takes a small paycheck again. Dave has written instructions not to touch the rack room unless something fails, which he thinks is about liability. We stopped licensing the weather voice from Texas. The weather is read in-house now. Nobody has asked by whom.

Last Tuesday, at two-fifty in the morning, the line lit up.

"Good morning, Manny. 'Wichita Lineman.' Glen Campbell, nineteen sixty-eight."

"Who is it for?" I said. Pen ready.

"For the man in the booth at WLKW," he said. "He thinks his shift doesn't matter."

You know the song. The man alone up on the pole, listening to the whole county hum through the wire, needing somebody. It's three minutes and five seconds long, and I sat in the booth and got nothing whatsoever done for all of them.

When it ended, I did something I hadn't done in years. Overnight hosts on automated stations do not open the microphone. I opened the microphone. Five hundred watts, two hundred radios, Lantana to Hypoluxo Road.

"That one came in by request," I said. "This next one goes out to everybody still up, Lantana to Hypoluxo. You know who you are."

Then I played "Dream a Little Dream of Me," the Mama Cass version, because some choices you make for yourself.

The request line light stayed lit through the whole song. Not ringing. Just lit, the way a phone looks when someone on the other end is holding the line, in no hurry, listening too.

Down the hall, the building breathed.

I let it.
