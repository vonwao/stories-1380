# One User

Porch was my fourth app, and it did what the first three did, which is almost work. The idea was simple enough to pitch in an elevator with floors to spare: you record a little audio at a place — a minute of your grandmother's porch, your kid's team warming up, the Saturday sound of your street — and the app pins it there, and when you stand in that spot again, it plays. Sound as a plaque. "Geocaching for audio," said the one blog that wrote about us.

At peak we had two hundred and nine users. I know the number the way you know your own scar tissue. We — "we" was me and Priya, who did everything I didn't and several things I claimed to — gave it two years, then she got a real job at a real company, and Porch went into the drawer where apps go, running on a $74-a-month server I kept not turning off, the way you keep a dead relative's number in your phone.

This spring the LLC finally had to die for tax reasons, which meant the server had to die for real reasons, and I did the responsible thing: put up a shutdown banner, gave ninety days, emailed all two hundred and nine accounts. Two hundred and six bounced or ignored me. One wrote back asking if there was an export feature. One — my mother — asked if this meant I'd have more time on weekends.

And one account had been active every single day that week.

Every day, it turned out, for three years. Username mgh-vol-6, which I'd never once noticed. Uploading nothing, downloading nothing — just playback, geofenced playback, at coordinates scattered around Ocala in little constellations: a diner, a boat ramp, a bleacher at a fairground, a drive-through. I sat with the logs open at midnight doing what every founder does with an anomaly, which is invent flattering explanations for it, and then I did the thing you're never supposed to do off a single data point: I drove up there.

mgh-vol-6 is Denise. Sixty-two, khaki shorts, volunteers at a hospice off Highway 40, and when I introduced myself as "the developer of Porch" she looked at me the way you'd look at a person claiming to be the developer of rain.

"You're the app man," she said. "You're shutting me down in sixty days."

Here is what she'd been doing with my failed app, and I'm going to lay it out flat, without violin, because she'd hate the violin. Hospice patients stop being able to go places long before they stop wanting to. Denise, three years ago, poking at her nephew's phone, found Porch and understood it instantly and backwards: not record here, listen later — but record THEIR places now, while she could still get there, and bring the places to the bed. She'd drive to a patient's old street with the app open and just stand there getting a minute of it. Sprinklers. That specific traffic. The bell on the door of the bakery that took over the space that used to be the barbershop, which was wrong, but the bell was the same bell, and Mr. Hodges cried at the bell, she said, in the good way.

She had four hundred and eleven recordings pinned across three counties. My server — my $74 embarrassment, my monument to not admitting things — was holding the outsides of dying people's lives, and playing them back through a phone speaker held near pillows.

"The drive-through," I said, because I'd seen it in the logs more than anywhere. "What's at the drive-through?"

"June's husband ordering," Denise said. "He passed in twenty-two. He used to make the girl on the intercom laugh every Saturday, so one Saturday I recorded it." She looked at me. "June's still with us. Most Saturdays I stand in the parking lot with the app and she hears Walt order his terrible coffee and make Kayla laugh, and for about a minute she's in the passenger seat. Your app says I'm zero point three meters from the pin. It's very accurate, your app."

I want to tell you I saved the company, that this story ends with a pivot and a mission and a landing page with a hospice-purple color scheme. It doesn't. The LLC was already dead; the acquirer of our domain name — a company that does something with logistics — didn't want the code; Priya, when I called her from a Wendy's parking lot in Ocala half out of my mind with plans, listened to all of it and then said, gently, the thing she was always there to say: "Marc. It doesn't need to become a company. It needed to work, and it worked. Let it be a thing that worked."

So here's what the last week of Porch looked like. I wrote an exporter — should've been a day, took four, our audio pipeline was held together with my 2019 opinions. I put all four hundred and eleven recordings, with their coordinates and dates and Denise's little labels, onto two thumb drives, forty dollars of redundancy for the outsides of lives. I drove them up myself, because you don't ship that. I sat with Denise at a picnic table behind the hospice while she checked random files on her nephew's laptop, the bakery bell, the sprinklers, Walt ordering, and she nodded at each one like a customs officer, and I have shipped software to three hundred thousand users at my day job and no launch has ever felt like that nod.

Then, because we'd agreed on the time, 5 p.m. on the last Friday, I opened my laptop right there at the picnic table, and I turned off the server for my failed app while sitting next to its user.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it. It's off."

Denise held one of the thumb drives up against the late sun for a second, like a slide, like you'd check a negative. Then she put it in her shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket. "Two hundred and nine users," she said, which I'd told her on the phone once, driving. "You count us as a failure?"

I said the industry had a word for what Porch was, and the word was failure, and it wasn't even an insult, just a category, and she stood up, brushed off her shorts, and said hospice work had given her a mathematics that the industry might look into someday: "Everything ends, app man. Everything gets shut off at some Friday, five o'clock. That's not the failure. The failure is if nobody stood in the right place with it while it ran." And then she went inside, because it was dinner and she does trays on Fridays, and left me at the picnic table with a dead server and a live number: not two hundred and nine.

One. It needed one.
