# The Deer (partial)

Marisol lost more than any of us and it never left a mark, which is why we could not forgive her.

The group meets Tuesdays in the annex room of St. Ambrose, the one with the accordion wall and the coffee urn that takes forty minutes to heat, so whoever grieves hardest still sets up chairs. There are name tags in a shoebox nobody has needed for two years. Gail facilitates. Gail has a voice like a warm bath and a laminated sheet of guidelines, and the first guideline is *there is no wrong way to grieve*, which we all recite and none of us believe. There are wrong ways. We keep a list without keeping it. For two years, Marisol has been the list.

Her husband, first, before my time. Then her mother the spring I joined. She'd tell it, whatever it was, in three or four sentences, in the same voice she used to report that the urn was acting up again. Then she'd shake — actually shake, a small tremor through the shoulders, ten seconds, like a dog coming out of a lake — and then she would ask Benny how his week was, and mean it.

I told the group about the shaking once, at the coffee after, and used the word *unsettling*, and got seven slow nods, and I want to be honest about how good those nods felt.

Last Tuesday she came in and said her brother had died. Sudden, his heart, in his truck in his own driveway in Maracay, and she'd been on the phone with the family all weekend about whether to fly down.

Gail leaned in with the bath voice. "Stay with that, Marisol. Take your time. Where do you feel it?"

"I felt it Saturday," Marisol said, not unkindly. "It sat on my chest all day, and I let it, and around evening it got up. We were done talking years ago, he and I. In a good way. There's nothing I owe it now."

"Grief isn't a debt," Gail said, "but sometimes when we say we're *done*—"

"You think I set it down too fast." She looked around the circle at us, patient, the urn ticking behind her. "You all think that. You've thought it for two years. You sit there with your losses in your laps, petting them, and when mine gets up and walks off you look at me like I drowned it."

Nobody said anything. I have a story about my wife that I have told that room eleven times. It's a good story. It has a pause in it, before the last line, and I know exactly how long the pause has to be, and that Tuesday, for the first time, sitting in the ring of chairs with the pause loaded and nowhere to put it, I heard how rehearsed I sound. Benny cried, which Benny does, which is honest at least. Marisol passed him the tissues like a woman passing bread.

In the parking lot afterward, scraping frost with my glove, I watched her sit in her car a long time before the headlights came on. Not crying. Not phoning. Just sitting, hands at the top of the wheel, looking at the annex door. Whatever she was doing in there, it was the only part of her I've ever seen that looked like work.
