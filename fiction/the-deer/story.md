# The Deer

Marisol lost more than any of us and it never left a mark, which is why we could not forgive her.

The group meets Tuesdays in the annex room of St. Ambrose, the one with the accordion wall and the coffee urn that takes forty minutes to heat, so whoever grieves hardest still sets up chairs. There are name tags in a shoebox nobody has needed for two years. Gail facilitates. Gail has a voice like a warm bath and a laminated sheet of guidelines, and the first guideline is *there is no wrong way to grieve*, which we all recite and none of us believe. There are wrong ways. We keep a list without keeping it. For two years, Marisol has been the list.

Her husband, first, before my time. Then her mother the spring I joined. She'd tell it, whatever it was, in three or four sentences, in the same voice she used to report that the urn was acting up again. Then she'd shake — actually shake, a small tremor through the shoulders, ten seconds, like a dog coming out of a lake — and then she would ask Benny how his week was, and mean it.

I told the group about the shaking once, at the coffee after, and used the word *unsettling*, and got seven slow nods, and I want to be honest about how good those nods felt.

Last Tuesday she came in and said her brother had died. Sudden, his heart, in his truck in his own driveway in Maracay, and she'd been on the phone with the family all weekend about whether to fly down.

Gail leaned in with the bath voice. "Stay with that, Marisol. Take your time. Where do you feel it?"

"I felt it Saturday," Marisol said, not unkindly. "It sat on my chest all day, and I let it, and around evening it got up. We were done talking years ago, he and I. In a good way. There's nothing I owe it now."

"Grief isn't a debt," Gail said, "but sometimes when we say we're *done*—"

"You think I set it down too fast." She looked around the circle at us, patient, the urn ticking behind her. "You all think that. You've thought it for two years. You sit there with your losses in your laps, petting them, and when mine gets up and walks off you look at me like I drowned it."

Nobody said anything. I have a story about my wife that I have told that room eleven times. It's a good story. It has a pause in it, before the last line, and I know exactly how long the pause has to be, and that Tuesday, for the first time, sitting in the ring of chairs with the pause loaded and nowhere to put it, I heard how rehearsed I sound. Benny cried, which Benny does, which is honest at least. Marisol passed him the tissues like a woman passing bread.

In the parking lot afterward, scraping frost with my glove, I watched her sit in her car a long time before the headlights came on. Not crying. Not phoning. Just sitting, hands at the top of the wheel, looking at the annex door. Whatever she was doing in there, it was the only part of her I've ever seen that looked like work.

The next Tuesday the annex was dark when I got there, and the urn was cold, and the chairs were still on the rack. In two years I had never once asked who plugged the urn in at six.

We set up badly, all of us at once, chairs scraping, and the coffee never got past lukewarm, and Gail opened by saying she wanted to name that Marisol's chair was empty tonight, and that we could hold both concern and respect for her process.

"Is she in Venezuela?" Benny asked.

"Her car's in her driveway," said Diane, whose loss is a daughter and who therefore outranks us all. "I drive past on my way to the pharmacy."

We were quiet in the way that isn't. Then Diane said what the room was petting. "I just worry that everything she never let herself feel is in that house with her. It doesn't go anywhere. It waits."

"I've been doing this fifteen years," Gail said, and for once the bath drained out of her voice and something older was under it. "I have seen *done*. I have seen it come back with interest. I buried one of mine who was done." She looked at the laminated sheet in her lap as if it had been written by someone she used to trust. Then guideline number six, continuity of care: somebody should check in. Not to process. Just eyes on her.

I said her house was on my way home. It is not on my way home.

She answered the door with a roll of packing tape on her wrist like a bracelet, and behind her the front room was half in boxes, and she looked at me for a moment the way you look at weather.

"They sent you," she said.

"They sent a card." I held it out. Gail's loops, seven signatures, a lily on the front. She read it on the porch, all the way through, and then walked it to the recycling bin at the side of the house and set it in, not angry, sorting it, the way you sort mail. Then she held the door open, which I had not expected.

The kitchen was warm and smelled of cinnamon and cardboard. A suitcase stood by the table with its mouth open. She put the kettle on without asking and I sat down and told her the thing I had driven over not to tell her.

"It was me. The word *unsettling*. About the shaking. I said it at coffee, two years ago, and it went around."

"I know," she said. She didn't turn from the stove. "One of you does the coffee run with the woman who cuts my hair. I heard it back in March, under the dryer, from a stranger." She turned around then. "Count the Tuesdays since March that you've seen me do it."

I counted. The counting took a while because I kept starting over, hoping.

"It won't come if someone in the room is taking notes," she said. "That's all it needs. Somewhere the dogs can't reach you. I had one room like that and you put a word on it." She said it without heat, which was worse. "I tried the car. That last night, in the lot, you watched me try. It never comes in the car."

"Marisol." Her name felt like borrowing something. "Last Tuesday you said it got up and walked off. Your brother."

"It got up," she said. "It didn't walk off. There's a difference, and the difference is sitting on my chest, and there is no room left where I can let it finish."

The kettle began its small argument with itself. She got down two cups and I watched her hands, which were steady, and I understood that steady was not the same as fine and that I had spent two years scoring her on the wrong test entirely.

"When I was nine," she said, "my brother hit a deer with the truck, outside Maracay, at dusk. Everyone screamed. He pulled over, and the deer was on the verge, on its side, and my mother said don't look, it's dying. Then it stood up. And it shook. Not like shivering — like something enormous had hold of it and was shaking the crash back out of it, the whole animal, a long time, longer than you'd think a body could. Then it stopped, and looked at nothing, and stepped into the trees like it had remembered an appointment. My brother wouldn't drive until it was gone. Forty cars honking behind us. He was seventeen and he did not care."

She poured. "Deer do that. All of them, every time, if they live. People are the only animal that can interrupt it. We interrupt it in each other. We call it *unsettling*." She set my cup down in front of me. "Your story has a pause in it."

I said nothing.

"Eleven times, Tom. You pause before the last line — *she said my name twice, like she was making sure of it* — and you look at the window. It's a beautiful line. Your wife isn't in it anymore." She sat down across from me with her own cup. "Were you there?"

I didn't answer, and she nodded like I had.

The kitchen ticked. The suitcase stood there with its mouth open. I said, "I could sit here. I don't have anywhere to be." And when she looked at me: "No bath voice. I won't ask where you feel it. I won't say anything at all."

She studied me a long time, and I watched her price it, letting the man who sold her shake to a room full of nodders be the one who watched it come back. It cost her. She paid it out slowly, like someone counting bills they'd hidden in a book.

"Don't touch me," she said. "And whatever it looks like, you don't call anyone. Swear it on your eleven times."

"I swear."

"Thursday I fly down. Not for the mass — the mass was Sunday. Somebody has to move his truck out of the driveway. My sister-in-law walks past it like it's asleep." She set her cup aside and put her hands loose in her lap. "That part hasn't gotten up yet. Maybe it doesn't."

Then for a while, nothing, long enough that I felt foolish, and the refrigerator hummed, and I looked at the floor to give her the room I'd promised.

It started in her shoulders, the small tremor I knew, and I thought, ten seconds. It did not stop at ten seconds. It went down her arms and into her hands and then it had all of her, her heels drumming the linoleum, her jaw, a sound coming out of her that was not crying and not words, and it went on. It looked like a woman being shaken by something I couldn't see, which I suppose is what it was. Three minutes. Four. My hand found my phone in my pocket, and I held the phone, and I kept my oath by the width of a swallowed breath, and it went on, and I understood why she needed the swearing.

Then it was over. Not tapering — over, like weather leaving a county. She sat still for a moment, wet-faced, loose, and then she got up and put the kettle back on, and turned around, and asked me how my week was. And meant it.

I told her the truth, which was that I'd eaten dinner standing over the sink every night since Christmas, and she laughed, and we drank the second cups talking about nothing holy, and when I left she stood in the doorway with the tape still on her wrist.

"Tell them whatever you like," she said. "You will anyway."

Tuesday I got to the annex at six and plugged in the urn. It ticks while it heats. I set out the chairs, and I went through it once out loud, alone, with the chairs watching, which tells you what I am.

Gail opened by saying that Marisol had decided to step away from group for a while, and that we were going to honor that. Diane nodded the way you nod at a verdict you'd have handed down yourself. Benny asked if she was okay, actually okay, and Gail said that was Marisol's to hold, and the urn ticked, and then Gail asked who would like to start.

I told the story about my wife. Twelfth time. It had never gone better; I could hear the room lean. I came to the pause and I let it open, wide as it would go, and then I did not say the line.

"I wasn't there," I said. "She told me to go home. She said I was hovering, and one of us should sleep, and I went home, and I slept fine, and the hospital called at ten past four. The part about my name — a nurse told me that. In a hallway, by the vending machines. I've never been sure she wasn't just being kind."

The silence had a different weight than the one I'd rehearsed. No nods came. I sat in the ring of chairs and felt the story leave me the way you feel a tooth leave — the roaring space where a thing you hated and needed had been, and no way ever to put it back, because now they had heard the true one and the beautiful one was spent. Seven faces arranged themselves the way ours had arranged at Marisol for two years. Diane looked at her lap. Gail's hand moved to the laminated sheet.

Benny got up, crossed the circle, and passed me the box of tissues like a man passing bread.

And Gail leaned in with the bath voice, and asked me where I felt it.
