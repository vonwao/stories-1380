# Dust Mites

The first one came out of the laptop fan and walked across my wrist.

It was smaller than an ant and bright blue. I thought it was a spark until it stopped beside my watchband and began pulling at a loose thread.

I blew on it. It held on.

I blew harder. It flattened itself against my skin, six little legs spread wide. Then it tore off a piece of thread and carried it back toward the laptop.

My build finished.

On the screen, the progress bar reached one hundred percent and the new convention display lit up on the workbench. Blue panels unfolded across it, each one holding the sample information I had typed:

WELCOME, GUEST.

CHOOSE YOUR FUTURE.

NO SELECTION MADE.

The blue thing disappeared into the vent.

I closed the laptop.

"No," I said.

Something clicked inside it.

I opened it again.

The display now said:

THREAD ACCEPTED.

I called Mara.

She answered over wind. "If this is about Thursday, I already said yes."

"Where are you?"

"Walking."

"Where?"

"Outside."

"I need you to come here."

"Is there blood?"

"No."

"Smoke?"

"No."

"Do you need my truck?"

"Maybe."

"That's smoke."

"Something came out of my computer."

She was quiet for a second. "An animal?"

"I don't know."

"Alive?"

"I don't know."

"Good. I'll be there in twenty."

Mara arrived in twelve minutes with hiking shoes on, one sock green and one sock gray. She worked as a set dresser for trade shows and small films, which meant her truck contained a folding palm tree, two wheelchairs, six feet of fake brick wall, and anything else a person might need without notice. She brought in a butterfly net and an orange.

"Why the orange?"

"Lunch."

"It's four thirty."

"Then I succeeded."

I showed her the thread on the screen.

"Could be a joke in the library."

"It came out of the fan."

"Show me."

"I can't summon it."

Mara put the orange beside the laptop and leaned over the keyboard.

"What did you add today?"

"Two interface libraries."

"What are those in English?"

"Boxes of code somebody else wrote."

"And you put both boxes in there?"

"They're compatible."

"Apparently not."

She took out a pocket magnifier. Mara carried one because she believed the world was always doing something small and criminal nearby.

The laptop clicked again.

Three blue specks came through the vent.

One went straight to the loose cuff of my sweatshirt. One climbed onto the orange. The third stood still on the aluminum case, turning in tiny, sharp movements.

Mara lowered the magnifier.

"Oh," she said.

"What?"

"That is an animal."

"It is not."

"Then why is it cleaning its face?"

The one on the orange rubbed its two front legs together.

Under magnification it looked made rather than born. Its body was a blue bracket with a clear bead at the center. The six legs ended in hooks. A row of symbols moved inside the bead too quickly to read.

The mite bit into the orange peel.

"Don't let it."

Mara moved the orange away. The mite went with it.

"Mara."

"I see it."

"Get it off."

"Why?"

"That's your lunch."

"It has good judgment."

The mite removed a perfect square of peel. It tucked the square beneath its body, folded its legs around it, and began to shake.

There were now two mites.

The new one was orange.

Mara looked at me.

"You saw that."

"Yes."

"It used the peel."

"Yes."

"Like material."

"Please stop sounding happy."

The blue mite and the orange mite touched front legs, then headed in opposite directions. One crossed the table toward the laptop. The other went into a bowl of keys.

We caught eleven in the first hour.

Mara developed the method. Place a glass over the mite, slide an index card underneath, turn the glass over, and cover it with a second card. I labeled each glass with the time and material.

4:51 BLUE / THREAD

4:58 ORANGE / PEEL

5:07 SILVER / KEY

5:12 BLACK / UNKNOWN, MAYBE COFFEE

We ran out of drinking glasses and moved to jars. The silver ones were the hardest to catch because they moved in straight lines and changed direction only at right angles. The orange ones were slower but climbed anything.

At six twenty, a red mite came out of the power strip.

It did not behave like the others. It ran in loops, touching every object on the workbench. Each time it returned to its starting point, another red mite appeared beside it.

"That's the other library," I said.

"How can you tell?"

"It keeps doing the same thing and calling it progress."

"You wrote it?"

"No."

"Then you can say that."

I unplugged the power strip. The red mites continued.

Mara watched them through the magnifier. "The blue ones make themselves from whatever they touch. The red ones copy themselves first and figure out the bodies later."

"That's not possible."

"I don't think possible is helping."

At seven, Nadia called.

Nadia ran the Home Tomorrow Expo, opening Friday at the Palm County Convention Hall. I was building the centerpiece for the entrance: a wall of forty small screens that asked visitors questions about the future and changed color according to their answers. The organizers called it the Living Consensus. I called it the Wall when Nadia was not around.

"How's my delight?" she asked.

"Stable."

"Stable isn't delight."

"It's running."

"We load in tomorrow at six. Sponsor walk-through Thursday at three. Please tell me the wall says their name now."

On the workbench, the sample display changed.

WELCOME, THREAD.

WELCOME, PEEL.

WELCOME, KEY.

"Their name is in the next build," I said.

Mara covered her mouth.

"You sound strange," Nadia said.

"I've been working."

"Sleep tonight. I need your nice personality tomorrow."

She hung up.

"Your nice personality?" Mara said.

"It appears at trade shows."

We put the laptop inside a clear storage bin and snapped the lid shut. Within ten minutes the blue mites had built a ladder out of dust and were working at the latch.

"Dust mites," Mara said.

"Don't name them."

"Too late."

She lifted the bin. The mites scattered, leaving behind a shape like a tiny fire escape.

"They use what is there," she said. "Lint, peel, metal, dust."

"Like a virus."

"Like everything alive."

"A squirrel doesn't copy itself when it touches my charger."

"Not while you're watching."

By midnight, we believed we had contained them.

We were wrong because we had only checked the workbench.

The blue mites had reached the kitchen.

They had cut squares from the sponge and built a bright green colony beneath the toaster. Silver ones moved through the silverware drawer in organized rows. The red ones had taken the pepper flakes and made a pulsing red knot behind the coffee maker.

When I switched on the overhead light, every mite froze.

"They hate light," I said.

Mara switched it off. They started moving.

"They prefer low power."

"That is not better."

We put lamps in every room. We used lint rollers along the baseboards. We vacuumed until the vacuum began displaying a blinking blue bracket on its battery indicator.

At two in the morning, Mara lay on the floor and watched mites carry a piece of cork into the wall.

"They're sorting by system," she said.

"They're ruining my apartment by system."

"Blue colony in the kitchen. Red colony near power. Silver ones on hardware. They're not random."

"Mold has preferences too. We kill mold."

"Do we?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Mara."

She sat up. "We could take them somewhere."

"Where?"

"Outside."

"So they can turn a tree into a user interface?"

"A jar, then."

"They're escaping jars."

"Not the big one in my truck."

"Why do you have a big jar in your truck?"

"Prop olives."

At three ten, I found a mite in my sock.

At three eleven, Mara went home.

I slept with the lights on.

The next afternoon we loaded the Wall into Mara's truck. Each screen went in a padded case. The control computer traveled inside a clear bin with a lamp clipped to its lid. I had removed both new libraries and rebuilt the program from the previous week's code.

Nothing moved.

At the convention hall, Nadia stood beneath a twenty-foot banner that showed a smiling family controlling their house from a tablet. The family had no charging cables, extension cords, or visible disagreements.

"There he is," she said. "Nice personality."

Mara unloaded the cases.

"And who is this?"

"Mara. Set work."

"Wonderful. I have six fake kitchen drawers that don't open."

"I can make them open."

"Please don't. Sponsor chose them."

We assembled the Wall across from a company demonstrating a refrigerator that ordered milk by itself. Nadia taped sponsor cards to the floor. Mara found a dying palm in the loading dock and placed it beside our display without permission.

At nine that night, the screens came on.

The Wall worked.

Nadia answered the first question:

WHAT SHOULD A HOME NOTICE?

She chose YOU.

The screens turned warm yellow. Her answer joined the sample answers moving across the display.

"I like it," she said.

"Good."

"Can it sparkle?"

"No."

"Thursday?"

"Still no."

"Ask the code people."

"I am the code people."

Mara made a small coughing sound behind the palm.

We left the convention hall at eleven. I made her stop the truck before the parking gate.

"Open the back."

"Why?"

"I heard something."

"The folding palm squeaks."

"Open it."

We found the first mite on the Living Consensus sponsor logo.

It was bright gold.

Mara put a jar over it.

The second mite was inside a screen. Then the third. A blue line moved across the whole Wall, case by case, although it was unplugged.

"They were in it before we left the apartment," she said.

"We take it apart."

"Here?"

"Now."

"You have forty screens."

"Help me."

We worked in the parking lot under a sodium lamp. No mites appeared in the control case. None appeared in the cables. But every screen had a tiny colored shape behind the glass.

Red. Blue. Silver. Green. Gold.

They had moved into the Wall.

My phone rang. Nadia.

"The refrigerator people say you left a drill."

"I have it."

"They say it's theirs."

I looked at the drill beside Mara's truck. Silver mites moved in and out of the battery vents.

"It might be."

"Are you all right?"

"No."

"Can you be all right by three tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

"Good. I believe in you."

Nadia hung up.

"She doesn't," Mara said.

"Nobody does at a convention."

We took the Wall to my apartment. I spread every screen across the living room floor and connected them.

The mites had rewritten the display.

Instead of the five questions I had designed, it asked one:

WHAT IS HOME MADE OF?

Below it moved the things they had used.

THREAD

PEEL

KEY

DUST

POWER

The gold mite had added SPONSOR.

"They understood the Wall," Mara said.

"They ate the Wall."

"It says more now."

"I have a walk-through in fourteen hours."

"Show Nadia."

"Show her what? Tiny self-copying program animals that live in materials? She wanted sparkle."

"They sparkle."

"She will cancel the contract."

"Then don't show her."

"You just said show her."

"I changed my mind."

Mara crouched beside the screens.

"Take the control computer away. Let them run without it."

"That could spread them into the hall."

"They're already in the hall."

We both looked at the drill.

"No," I said.

"We can contain the display. Glass barrier, bright perimeter lights."

"No."

"Then what?"

"Heat."

She stood.

"Heat?"

"The laptop manual says safe operating up to ninety-five Celsius. The mites came from the fan. We close the computer in the thermal cabinet at the shop, raise it slowly, see what happens."

"You want to cook them."

"I want my apartment back."

"They're not in your computer now. They're in forty screens and a drill and probably my truck."

"Then we heat those."

"My truck?"

"Not the truck."

"Good."

"Mara, I have to stop them."

"You want to stop the part that inconveniences you. You don't even know what the rest is."

"It came from my code."

"You downloaded the code."

"Onto my computer."

"That's not parenthood."

"I did not say it was."

"You're acting like a bad father."

"To dust."

"To something."

She took her backpack from the chair.

"Where are you going?"

"Hiking."

"It's one in the morning."

"I'm angry."

"That's not safer in the dark."

"The trail has lights."

"Take the orange."

"You let them eat it."

She left without the orange.

At three, I put one blue mite in a small laboratory warming box I used for adhesive. It walked in circles as the temperature rose. At forty degrees it slowed. At fifty it stopped.

I turned the heat off.

When the box cooled, the mite did not move.

I sat at the kitchen table until morning.

At ten, Nadia called to say the sponsor walk-through had moved to one. At ten fifteen, the convention hall called about a problem with the refrigerator display.

The refrigerator had ordered forty-eight gallons of milk.

At ten thirty, Mara returned in the truck. Her hair was wet with sweat and both socks were now gray with dirt.

She put a jar on the table.

Inside, blue mites moved across a piece of limestone.

"You took them to the trail?"

"I took the jar. They never left it."

"You could have dropped it."

"I didn't."

"They could get out."

"They didn't."

"This is not intuition. It's recklessness."

"And cooking one alone in a box is science?"

I did not answer.

She saw the warming box.

"Did it die?"

"I don't know what dead means for it."

"Convenient."

"I turned it off."

"After."

"Yes."

She took a chair and sat.

For a while neither of us spoke.

The dead mite moved.

One leg first. Then another. It dragged itself to the edge of the box. Its clear center was cloudy.

Mara slid an index card under it.

"What happened?" she asked.

"It changed."

"No. What did it use?"

The mite's six legs were no longer blue. They were clear and brittle.

The warming box had a silicone pad on the bottom. A tiny square was missing.

"It used the box," I said.

The mite stood. Under the magnifier, the cloud inside it separated into symbols.

It walked to the edge of the card and stopped in front of the jar from the trail.

The blue mites inside stopped too.

All at once, they began building.

They used grit from the limestone to make a gray platform. On it they placed three pieces of blue material, then one clear piece. It looked like a pattern. Or a warning. Or neither.

"Two libraries," Mara said. "Maybe they're trying to talk across them."

"By making a loading dock?"

"You make boxes on a screen."

"Interfaces."

"Boxes."

At noon, we carried the jar, the warming box, and forty infested screens back to the convention hall.

Nadia met us at the loading entrance.

"The refrigerator company is furious."

"It wasn't our drill."

"They know that now. Their drill is ordering eggs."

Behind her, the exhibition hall was making small noises. Booth lights changed color without being touched. A massage chair recited the Wi-Fi password. The automatic curtains at Future Bedroom opened and closed in a steady rhythm.

Mites moved through the carpet like colored sand.

Nadia watched a red one climb her shoe.

"Is that the sparkle?"

"No."

Mara said, "Sort of."

The sponsor representatives arrived at twelve fifty-five.

We put the Wall at the entrance and turned off its control computer. Around it Mara placed four bright work lamps. On a white pedestal in front, we set the two jars and the warming box.

"What's the containment plan?" Nadia asked.

"The lights."

"And if that fails?"

"The jars."

"And if that fails?"

Mara handed her the butterfly net.

The sponsors came through the doors.

The Wall lit without power.

WHAT IS HOME MADE OF?

The blue mites filled the first ten screens with words:

THREAD. PEEL. KEY. DUST.

The red mites copied the words across the other screens until every panel was full. Then the silver colony cut clean paths between them. The green mites from the kitchen sponge made something like a border. The gold ones ate the printed sponsor logo and rebuilt it in the center.

Nadia whispered, "They made it bigger."

"They do that."

One of the sponsor men stepped close. "How does it know what material it's on?"

"It uses the material," Mara said.

"Can people interact with it?"

"Please don't touch it," I said.

He touched it.

The mites streamed toward his finger. He pulled back. They stopped at the edge of the glass.

The sponsor woman laughed.

Soon everyone was laughing, not because it was a joke but because there was nothing else ready in the body for what they were seeing.

The Wall changed again.

WHAT SHOULD A HOME NOTICE?

YOU appeared, Nadia's answer from the night before.

Then the mites added:

EVERYTHING ELSE.

The sponsor woman turned to me. "Did you write that?"

Mara waited.

"No," I said.

Behind us, the refrigerator announced that two hundred gallons of milk would arrive between two and four.

The sponsor man looked around.

"Is that part of it?"

"No," Nadia said. "That's a separate delight."

We opened the expo Friday morning.

The official brochure called the mites an emergent material interface. The sponsor added a clear shield around the Wall and hired two people to stand beside it with nets. Children spent twenty minutes watching the colonies choose between lint and cracker crumbs. Adults asked what the commercial application was.

I said we didn't know.

That answer bothered them.

On Sunday evening, after the last visitor left, we dismantled the display. Most of the mites had entered the Wall. We moved all forty screens into a climate-controlled storage room that Nadia found at the university. A biologist signed for them and asked us not to use the word animal until she had funding.

The infected refrigerator stayed at the convention hall. It had begun cancelling its own milk orders.

Mara and I went back to my apartment with lint rollers, lamps, and the big prop-olive jar.

We found no colonies under the toaster. No mites in the silverware. The red knot behind the coffee maker was gone.

"They moved to the event," I said.

"Most of them."

"You saw one?"

"No."

"Then why say most?"

She picked up the orange from the workbench. It had dried hard and light. A small square was missing from the peel.

"Because life is rude," she said.

She split the orange with her thumbs. It was brown inside.

We threw it away.

Monday morning, I opened the clean version of my project and started removing the two libraries for good.

The code would not run.

One dependency was missing. I followed the error through six folders until I found a new file I had not created.

It was called `home.js`.

Inside was one line:

```javascript
export const madeOf = ["what is there"];
```

Something bright blue walked out from under the space bar, looked at me, and went back in.
