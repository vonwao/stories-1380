# The Algorithm Said No (partial)

They denied my rental application on a Tuesday at 4:52 in the afternoon, which I later found out is eight minutes before their office closes. So by the time you read the email, take a breath, and call — voicemail. Somebody designed that. Honestly, I have to respect it.

I should back up. The apartment was nothing special. Second floor, parking lot view, a pool with a sign listing fourteen rules. But it was ten minutes from everything in my life, and the application was $150, non-refundable, and I make a decent living fixing other people's software, so I wasn't worried. They wanted a background check. Fine. A credit check. Fine. A letter explaining a late payment from six years ago — and honestly? I liked that part. I like tests. I wrote them a beautiful letter. I explained the whole thing: the bank merger, the autopay that died quietly in the merger like a houseplant, the $34 that arrived nine days late in 2019.

Denied.

No reason given. So Wednesday morning I called, and got an actual human, whose name was Brianna, and who was never the villain of this story. Brianna was pleasant the way a closed door is pleasant when somebody paints it a nice color.

"Unfortunately I'm not able to share the specifics of the decision," she said.

"Okay, but you have them. The specifics. They exist and you're looking at them."

"I'm not able to share them."

"Who decided? Maybe I can talk to that person."

"It's not a person. Applications are evaluated by our screening system."

"Okay," I said. "Can I talk to the algorithm?"

There was a pause. And it wasn't an annoyed pause. It was the pause of a woman consulting a script and finding, for the first time in her career, a blank page.

"...No?" she said. Like a question. Like she was asking someone above her, and there was no one above her, there was only the algorithm.

That's when I got interested. You have to understand — and my ex-girlfriend would want me to tell you this — that this is a defect in my personality. A normal person eats the $150 and applies somewhere else. I heard *there's a rule and nobody will say it out loud* and something in the back of my brain lit up like a pinball machine paying out. Somewhere in that office was a sentence about me, written by a machine, that no human being was allowed to read to me. I didn't even want the apartment anymore, particularly. I wanted the sentence.

But I also needed a place to live. So Thursday I applied to a different complex — Sandpiper Cove, palm trees on the brochure, no sandpipers, no cove. Different name, different pool rules, $175 this time. I paid it. I attached the beautiful letter, which I'd revised. I'd made it shorter, warmer, more humble. A letter you'd approve out of sheer decency.

Denied. 4:52 PM. Friday.

I sat in my car in the Sandpiper Cove parking lot, reading the email twice, and then I did what I do when software misbehaves, which is stop yelling at the screen and go read what's actually running. I looked up who owned the place. Sandpiper Cove was managed by a company called Brightline Residential. So, out of curiosity — and this is the moment the week stopped being a bad week and became a project — I looked up the first complex.

Brightline Residential.

Same company. Same algorithm. Same 4:52. I'd paid the same machine twice to reject me, $325 total, and it had used the second $175 to do it faster, because this time it already knew me.

I got out of the car. There was a leasing-office window with a young guy behind it, and above him a banner that said NOW WELCOMING NEW RESIDENTS, and I stood there in the heat doing the math on how many complexes in the county Brightline managed.

The guy slid the window open. "You here to apply?"

"Give me one second," I said. "I'm deciding something."
