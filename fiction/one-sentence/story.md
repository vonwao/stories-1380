# One Sentence

The fire had been burning since moonrise, and nobody had lit it.

There were six of them around it, on the stone shoulder of the mountain, and below them, a switchback down, stood a hut with one window, dark. A boy was asleep in it. He was not really a boy — twenty-six, twenty-seven — but from where they sat, everyone looked young. Outside the hut door, leaning against the wall where he'd dropped it, was a rucksack of books with the corners worn round.

"Six books," said Franklin, who had gone down to look. "I counted. We are all of us in that bag. Carried up a mountain, gentlemen. On a man's back, in July."

"That is not reading," Thoreau said. "That is penance."

"It's how he prays," Franklin said. "Look at the spines. Broken in the same three places a spine breaks when a man reads the same pages over and over, hoping they'll say something new." He sat back down and produced, from somewhere, paper and a pencil stub, because he was the kind of man who always had them. "So. We have until sunrise, as I understand it. I propose we leave him a sentence."

"Why words?" said Marcus Aurelius.

"Because words are what he trusts. You don't hand a drowning man a stone because you personally prefer stones."

"I wrote words every night of a war," Marcus said. "To myself. Notes, lists, corrections. I never once wrote a sentence that opened anything. The writing was the drainage, not the spring."

"Then help me fail better," Franklin said, pleasantly. "One sentence, on the opening of the heart, and we have until the light comes over that ridge."

Nobody asked who had set the task. They all seemed to know, the way you know things on a mountain at night, and none of them said it out loud, and the fire burned on wood that nobody had gathered.

Yogananda was smiling into the flames. "One sentence is a good discipline," he said. "My teacher could do it. He once repaired a man's whole life with nine words, and two of them were the man's name."

"What were the other seven?" Franklin asked, pencil ready.

"They would not work for this boy," Yogananda said. "That is the trouble with the great sentences. They are fitted, like a key. You are proposing to leave a key without ever seeing the lock."

"I've seen the lock," Thoreau said. "The lock is that bag. He has read six accounts of the open heart and now he can quote everything and feel nothing, and he has come up a mountain to get away from the quoting. The kindest sentence we could leave him is: burn the books. Present company included."

"You wrote two of the books," Franklin said.

"I stand by the burning."

"You would," Franklin said. "You are the only man I ever met who moved to the woods and then wrote to everyone about the silence. Three chairs in that cabin of yours, Henry. One for solitude, two for friendship, three for society. You counted visitors like a man keeping accounts."

"And you kept actual accounts," Thoreau said. "A ledger of virtues. Thirteen of them, with little checkmarks. You tried to open the heart the way a man balances a shop, and I notice humility had to be added later, at the end, like a correction."

"It was a correction," Franklin said. "A friend told me I was insufferable. I wrote it down. That's the whole method, if anyone's asking — you hear the true thing, and instead of arguing, you write it down."

Gröning had not spoken yet. He sat nearest the fire with his hands on his knees, a small man with his collar open, and when he spoke it was the way a man speaks about the weather he can see coming.

"The boy is not sick," he said. "He is blocked. It is not the same thing."

"Explain the difference," Marcus said.

"A sick man, something must be done for him. A blocked man, something must stop being done." He turned his palms up, resting them on his knees, and it was strange how much the gesture looked like Lenore's, or hers like his, or like everyone's who has ever sat that way facing something they trust. "There is a stream. It flows all the time, also right now, also through this fire, also through that hut. The heart does not have to be opened the way you open a door, with work, with a shoulder against it. It has to stop being held shut. That is all I have ever taught anybody. You do not do. You let."

"Letting is a technique," Yogananda said gently. "You teach people how to sit, how to turn their palms, when to tune in — morning and evening, yes? That is method, my friend. You have a method and you are shy about it. I am not shy about mine. The heart is not only a metaphor; there is an actual center in the chest, and there are actual practices, and they work the way a lever works, for anyone who will do them. God is not moved by our moods. God is lawful. Practice is how you address the law."

"And if the boy practices for thirty years with his jaw set?" Gröning said. "I have seen the ones who practice like that. They arrive exactly where they started, only tired."

"And I have seen the ones who wait for the stream with their mouths open," Yogananda said, "for thirty years, in the rain."

It was the closest two of them came to quarreling, and it was close, because each was the other's missing half and both of them knew it, which is the only kind of quarrel worth staying up for.

Marcus put a branch on the fire that was not needed. "You are all describing the door," he said. "None of you has said what comes through it. I will tell you what comes through it. Everything. That is the problem with the heart, gentlemen, and I notice nobody says it to the young. Open it and you do not get to choose. The love comes through, and the plague comes through, and the letter with the seal you recognize comes through." He stopped. The fire ticked. "I buried children. More of them than I will say numbers about, because the numbers make it sound arithmetical. Each one was a door I could have closed afterward, and there were nights the closing had arguments on its side. Philosophy did not close them. That is not what it was for, whatever they say about us. It taught me to stand in the doorway. Upright, if possible. That is all the Stoa ever was — posture, for the doorway."

Nobody said anything for a while. Below them the hut's window had the particular darkness of a room where someone is genuinely asleep, the darkness with breath in it.

Then Jesus, who had been tending the fire the whole time — moving a coal here, a branch there, the way a man does who has decided the fire is his job so his hands have something honest to do — spoke for the first time.

"Tell me where you were," he said. "Each of you. When yours opened. Not what you concluded afterward. Where you were, and who was there."

"That's not a sentence for the boy," Franklin said.

"I asked anyway."

Franklin looked at the paper in his hand for a moment, and then he told it, in fewer words than he had used all night. "Philadelphia. Seventeen thirty-six. My son Francis was four years old, and the smallpox came through, and I had written in favor of inoculation — publicly, in my own paper — and I had not yet inoculated him. He was recovering from a flux; we were waiting for a stronger week. He died on my waiting." He folded the paper in half, once, evenly. "People said, at least it wasn't the inoculation that killed him, you'd never have forgiven yourself. They meant it kindly. But it opened on the guilt, not the grief, gentlemen — that's the part I'd tell him if I told anybody. It has a hinge of guilt on it and it has never once closed since, and every kindness I have done in fifty years walked in through that door."

"Concord," Thoreau said. "January. My brother John cut himself shaving with a razor, a nothing cut, and eleven days later he died of the lockjaw, in my arms, in our house. And twelve days after that my own jaw seized. Sympathetic, the doctors called it. No wound on me anywhere. I sat in a chair unable to eat while my body grieved on its own schedule, because I had refused to do it on mine." He looked at the fire. "I went to the pond two years later. People think I went to be alone. I went because the woods were the only place big enough to leave the door open in."

"Calcutta," Yogananda said. "I was eleven. My mother died while I was away from her, and they did not tell me at first — I knew on the train; something in the train knew. I beat on heaven's door about it for years. Years. I was not gentle and I was not fair. And the beating became knocking, and the knocking became a practice, and the practice became my life. So when I say method, Bruno, understand what I am telling you. My method is grief that learned to keep appointments."

"A camp in Russia," Gröning said. "Prisoner of war. The men were not dying of hunger, or not only. They were dying of the closed thing — you could watch it close in a man, two days, three, like ice over a pond, and then the hunger finished what the closing started. I had nothing. No bread to give, no medicine, nothing to do. And when there was finally nothing to do, something began coming through me that I did not make. That is all. That is my whole biography. I found out what I was when I was of no use."

They looked at Marcus. He shook his head slowly, not refusing — locating.

"A bed," he said at last. "Which one, it doesn't matter; there were many beds and it was always the same opening. But the one I keep: a slave girl, I never learned her name properly, holding my son — the last hour of him. She had no philosophy. She hadn't read anything; there was nothing in her head we would call preparation. And she held him better than I did. Her heart did the thing mine was still asking permission to do." He turned his hand over, as if the memory sat in the palm. "I was the most educated man in the world that night, and I was outranked."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, wind went through the pines on the dark side of the ridge and didn't reach them.

"And you," Franklin said quietly, because somebody had to. "Where were you, when yours opened?"

"A garden," Jesus said. He moved a coal. "It opened by breaking. I don't recommend the method or apologize for it."

And then he stood, and brushed off his knees, and walked down the switchback into the dark, and was gone so simply that for a moment nobody was sure they had seen him go.

They worked on the sentence while he was away, because the ridge behind them was beginning to be a different kind of dark.

Franklin went first: "Do one kindness before noon and another before sleep, and let the heart keep its own ledger." Thoreau said the boy would checkmark himself to death by Michaelmas, and Franklin, to his credit, wrote the objection down.

Thoreau offered: "Go far enough from the crowd and you will hear it open." Yogananda pointed down the switchback and said, softly, that the boy had already gone far enough from the crowd to be sleeping alone on a mountain with a bag of books, and it hadn't opened, and Thoreau looked at the hut for a long moment and withdrew the sentence himself, which cost him something.

Yogananda tried: "Sit every day until the stillness sits in you." Marcus said it was a good sentence and also a set of instructions, and the boy had six hundred pages of instructions rubbed soft at the corners already.

Gröning said: "Do not hold it shut. It wants to come." Franklin asked what "it" was, and Gröning said, "He will know," and Marcus said, "They never know. That is why they climb mountains," and Gröning nodded, agreeably, unbothered, like a man who has heard the objection before and has seen it not matter.

Marcus went last: "Love what comes through the door, since it will come anyway." Even he grimaced at it. "It sounds like surrender," he said. "It isn't. But it sounds like it, and the boy would hear the wrong thing." Franklin drew a line under all five and stared at the page.

That was when they noticed Jesus had been gone a long time.

He came back up the switchback carrying nothing, and sat down and put his hands toward the fire, and Franklin said, "You've been gone an hour."

"His fire was out," Jesus said. "He sleeps cold, your reader. And the blanket had come off his shoulder."

"You might have woken him," Marcus said, and there was no criticism in it; there was something more like envy. "You were the one of us he'd want to see. You might have told him everything."

"He needs sleep," Jesus said. "He's been reading."

The light came the way it comes on mountains, all at once and from underneath. The fire, which nobody had lit, went to embers politely, as though excusing itself. Franklin looked at his page, five sentences and a line under them, and turned it toward the others, and the paper was suddenly the wrong size for what it had been asked to hold — anyone could see that in the new light.

"Gentlemen," he said. "We are out of night."

"Leave the page," Thoreau said. "All five. Let him see the crossings-out. It's more honest than any of them alone."

"Leave nothing," Gröning said. "He came up blocked. He will go down different. It is already moving; I felt it start when the fire was mended." He stood and turned his palms down, done. "Not everything that is given has to be signed."

They went their ways off the shoulder of the mountain the way the dark goes off a field — no moment you could point to, and then gone, and whether Franklin left the page or burned it is between him and the fire.

The boy woke late, past eight, with the sun already through the one window and lying warm on the floorboards. His fire was banked and glowing, which puzzled him, because he had no memory of banking it, and had in fact fallen asleep resigned to a cold morning as a kind of punishment for nothing in particular. The blanket was tucked to his chin. His water, when he drank it in the doorway, tasted the way water tastes when you have slept all the way down for the first time in a year.

He did not open a book with breakfast. He noticed that the way you notice a sound stopping — a thing had been running in him so long it had become silence, and now it was actually silent, which was different, and better, and he stood in the doorway a while trying to remember what he had dreamed, and couldn't, and it didn't feel like losing anything.

Then he packed the six books, and swung the whole library onto his back, and went down the mountain. It weighed what it weighed. He carried it easily.
