# The Undreaming, Episode 1: Tío Feo (partial)

"Since when do I have a daughter?"

My sister said it the way you'd correct a wrong number. Not upset. A little amused. In the background I could hear her TV and her dogs and the specific Tuesday-night sounds of her kitchen, all of it completely normal, which was the problem.

"Ana. Put Vale on."

"Who?"

"Valentina." I was laughing a little myself now, the way you do when you're still sure it's a joke, when the joke is just running long. "Your kid. Six years old, this tall, thinks I'm ugly."

"Christoph," my sister said — and she used my whole name, which she does maybe once a year, "have you been sleeping?"

The dogs barked. The TV mumbled. Somewhere in that kitchen was the drawer where Vale kept her markers, second from the bottom, because at six the good drawers are all still assigned by treaty. Tuesday nights she answers my call herself, says "Tío Feo" — Ugly Uncle, a title she invented at four and has enforced since with the discipline of a federal agency — and we do our business. School gossip. Dog news. One knock-knock joke, always terrible on purpose.

"You're scaring me a little," Ana said, kindly. That was the worst part. The kindness.

I told her I was tired, that it was a work thing, a mixed-up name, and I got off the phone and stood in my kitchen for a while. Then I called my mother, because when reality has a discrepancy in it, you check it against the person who's been auditing your life since birth.

My mother went very quiet. Then she said, "Why do you keep doing this to Ana?" — *keep* doing, like there was a history, like there were incidents — and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice you use on a man standing somewhere high.

Okay. What I did next, I'm a little proud of, because it turns out I'm the kind of person who debugs. I didn't cry — that came later, and it stayed. That first night I got a legal pad and I made two columns: THINGS I CAN PROVE and THINGS I REMEMBER.

Things I remember: six years of a person. Her whole vocabulary. The way she yelled "feo!" at the end of a voicemail like a trumpet.

Things I can prove — and I looked, really looked, the way I'd audit a database at work. Her school's website: no Valentina. Ana's photos: I scrolled six years back on my phone, and it was seamless. Not deleted — *recomposed*. Christmas 2023 had the same table, the same tablecloth, the same tired uncles, and one fewer chair, and the picture was not missing anyone, because you can't be missing from a world that never had you in it. Whoever did this was thorough in a way that scared me more than any missing chair would have. Deletion leaves holes. This had no holes.

Except one. Column A had exactly one entry that was also physical: a voicemail from March. Fourteen seconds. She sings happy birthday in two languages and gets both wrong and yells the word at the end.

It's still there. I can't explain why the voicemail survived when six years of photographs got rewritten. What I did about it: I played it every night, on headphones, with the lights off, like another man would drink. And every night it felt a little heavier — and I know how that sounds, a sound file getting heavier — but I'm telling you it had *weight*, like something I was holding up out of water, and my arms were getting tired, and I didn't dare put it down, because I was starting to suspect that putting it down was exactly what the world was waiting for me to do.

Nine days after the phone call, I ended up at the playground on Dane Street again. Two blocks from my building. I kept arriving there without ever once deciding to go.

There was a woman on the bench by the swings. Sixties, heavy, brown cardigan, eating sunflower seeds out of her coat pocket like someone waiting for a bus in no particular century.

"You can still hear her," the woman said. Not a question. She didn't look up.

My heart went off like I'd been caught shoplifting.

"Sit down," she said, and patted the bench. "You've been carrying that standing up for nine days. No wonder you look terrible."
