# Sobada

The complaint came in like they all come in: typed into the county portal at 11 p.m. by somebody angry enough to find the portal. "Unlicensed massage business operating out of residential kitchen, Davis Road area, cash only, cars at all hours." Filed under my queue, because Lake Worth west of Dixie is mine, has been mine for nine years, every illegal shed and unpermitted fence of it.

I know what a massage complaint usually is, so I sat on it a week, and then a Tuesday came up light and I drove out. Yellow duplex, clean yard, crotons fighting a hibiscus for the good corner. Three cars out front. One of them was my mother's Corolla.

I sat in the county truck across the street for a long time, engine off, and did some math I'd been avoiding for about two years: my mother's knees. The way she'd stopped talking about her knees. My mother is seventy-six and Venezuelan and there is an entire category of things she has decided her son the county officer is better off not knowing, and I was parked in front of the category.

She came out at ten fifteen walking like a woman with new knees, sixty years old, tops. Drove off without seeing me, or without letting me see her see me, which in my family is a different and more advanced maneuver.

The door had one of those paper signs that means nothing and everything: BENDICIONES. The woman who opened it was in her seventies, small, hands like tree roots, and she looked at my county shirt for exactly as long as it deserved and then past it at me.

"You're Yolanda's boy," she said. Not a question. "The one with the county. She has a photo. Come in, I have coffee, I know why you're here."

Her name is Reina. Inside there was a massage table from Amazon in what should have been the dining room, a rack of clean towels, a shelf of Agua de Florida and Vicks and rosaries, and a kitchen that smelled like my grandmother's kitchen in Maracay so exactly that my chest did something unprofessional. She does sobadas. If you don't know what that is, I can't fully help you; the county form calls it "massage services," which is like calling your grandmother "a food services provider." It's the thing the women in my family did for knots and grief and babies turned the wrong way and men who wrecked their backs being proud. Her mother did it, her grandmother did it. She showed me her hands like you'd show credentials, and honestly, compared to the laminated massage-school certificates I inspect in strip malls, they were credentials.

"Doña Reina," I said, "somebody complained. When somebody complains, a file opens, and the file doesn't close by itself. You're running a business without an occupational license, in a residential zone, without a state massage license, which is a bigger one, that one's Tallahassee, not me."

"I don't charge," she said, and we both looked at the coffee can on the shelf with the twenties folded in it, and she had the grace to shrug. "People are grateful. I can't control gratitude."

I've heard that exact construction from tow-lot operators and I've heard it from this woman with my grandmother's kitchen, and the sentence is the same and it is not the same, and nine years in code enforcement has made me the world's least willing expert on the difference. The tow-lot guy is monetizing a loophole. Reina is what the loophole was clumsily trying to describe.

I asked who complained. She told me without me telling her I couldn't tell her: the new massage franchise on Jog Road had sent a girl around asking her prices. "I told her I have no prices," Reina said. "This confused everybody."

Here's what the statute gives me: warn, cite, escalate. Here's what the statute does not have a field for: my mother's knees, or the fact that half this woman's clients are the housekeeping staff of the very gated communities whose HOAs generate a third of my queue, women whose insurance covers nothing they can pronounce, or the smell of that kitchen.

I wrote the notice. I wrote it, I posted it, I did my job. Thirty days to cease commercial activity, first notice, smallest violation class I could defensibly file, which any of my colleagues would tell you is not enforcement, it's a mercy dressed as enforcement, and any of them would also tell you thirty days becomes sixty the moment the file sits under other files, and files sit. I know exactly what I did. I built her a delay out of the same bureaucratic sediment I spend my life digging other people out of, and it is the only time in nine years the machine has felt good in my hands, and I don't fully trust that feeling and don't need to.

While I taped it to the door she watched me the way you watch weather, and then she said, "Your mother's left knee is bone. She needs the surgery she's afraid of. What I do only buys her Tuesdays. Tell her to stop being a burra and I'll still see her after."

"You can't see her after. That's what the paper says."

"The paper says thirty days," said Doña Reina, who has clearly survived governments with more ambition than mine. "In thirty days, mijo, you'll be the one with the back. Nine years in that truck? You sit like a question mark. Come Thursday. Bring the complaint form, I'll fill it out on you myself."

I drove back to the office and closed out four fence violations like a man getting his story straight — and here is where I'm supposed to tell you what I decided, and the truth is the thirty days aren't up. The file is under some files. My mother called Sunday and talked about everything except her knees, fluently, for an hour; there's a surgeon's appointment in November she thinks I don't know about, so whatever Reina said to her worked, which is more than my notice has ever done taped to anything.

And Thursday I was out that way anyway, on a legitimate call, a shed. That's what I'd write if there were a form for it. I was out that way anyway.
