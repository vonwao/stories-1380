# The Shrine That Wasn't There

The woman put two pictures on Simón's desk.

One was the brochure he had made in March. It showed a white chapel on a green ridge, though the chapel was in Portugal and the ridge was three valleys north of Mérida. Simón had joined them on his laptop. The light did not match if you knew what to look for.

The other was an old photograph with a scalloped edge. A white building stood behind six children and two women. The ridge was hard to see. A thumb had covered part of the lens.

The woman pointed between the pictures. “Which is yours?”

She spoke Spanish carefully. Not badly. Carefully.

Simón picked up the old photograph. On the back, in blue ink, someone had written: Santa Brígida del Nublado, junio de 1954.

He put it down.

Simón asked, “Where did you get this?”

She said her mother had kept it in a cookbook.

He asked which cookbook. She asked why that mattered.

It did not. He needed another question while he looked at the saint's name.

The woman took off her glasses and cleaned them with a square of cloth. She had already placed a walking stick against his filing cabinet. It was better than either of his.

The woman recited his name and guide's license number as if entering them into evidence. Simón confirmed both.

Then she asked, “And Santa Brígida?”

Outside, a bus changed gears on the hill. Simón waited until it passed.

Simón said, “There are local names that don't appear in church records.”

The woman opened his brochure and pointed to a paragraph near the bottom.

She read aloud, “A widowed beekeeper carried the saint's image through a snowstorm in 1911.”

Simón called it oral history. The woman said there had never been a recorded snowstorm on that ridge.

“Snow is sometimes a broad word in mountain stories,” he said.

She asked, “And the beekeeper?”

“Also broad.”

She put her glasses on. “My name is Ingrid Weiss. I have paid you eight hundred euros.”

“Half the fee,” Simón said.

“Yes. I will pay the other half when you take me here.” She touched the old photograph.

Simón turned it toward the window. The paper was real. The ink looked old. He was not an expert on either, which meant nothing useful.

Simón said, “Mrs. Weiss, there may not be a here.”

Ingrid answered, “Then this should be easy. We go nowhere, and we come back.”

“The route is two days.”

“Your website says one.”

“My website is optimistic.”

“Your website is a crime scene.”

He liked her a little then. This was unhelpful.

“I can refund you,” he said.

He could refund about one hundred and forty euros. The rest had gone to an old rescue debt and his daughter's school account.

Ingrid looked at him long enough to do the arithmetic without knowing the numbers.

“Six tomorrow morning,” she said. “You may lie to me one more time tonight if you need to get it out of your system.”

She took the old photograph and left his brochure on the desk.

Simón waited until he heard her walking stick reach the stairs. Then he called Elías.

Simón said, “I need the good mule.”

Elías laughed before Simón explained anything.

At six the next morning, Ingrid was standing outside Elías's shed with a red suitcase large enough to require its own mule.

Elías walked around it once. “The mule says no.”

Ingrid said, “The mule has not seen it.”

“She has an imagination.”

La Ministra stood in the yard eating the corner of a feed sack. She was a dun mule with a dark stripe down her back and one ear bent at the tip. Elías had named her during an election year. He no longer remembered which minister she was meant to insult.

Ingrid opened the suitcase. She removed a second pair of shoes, a hair dryer, three hardback books, and a folded blue dress.

“You were bringing a dress?” Simón asked.

“I did not know what kind of fraud you were operating.”

She put the books and the hair dryer in Elías's hands. She kept the dress.

Inside the shed, ropes and old tack hung from nails. Above the workbench was an ice axe with a dark wooden shaft. A band of copper had been folded around a split near the handle and fixed with two crooked rivets. Simón's grandfather had made the repair from a piece of pipe before Simón was born. Elías said the axe brought luck, which was why he never took it down.

Elías reached for a coil of rope and knocked the axe loose. Simón caught it before it struck the bench.

“Careful,” he said. “That was my grandfather's.”

“Your grandfather used his tools,” Elías said. “You worship them.”

Simón set the axe back on its hooks. Ingrid was bent over her suitcase, moving candied ginger from a plastic bag into a flat metal tobacco tin.

Elías tightened the packs on La Ministra while Simón checked Ingrid's coat, water, gloves, and boots. Her equipment was good. Her knees were less easy to inspect.

“When did you last walk the upper section?” she asked.

“April.”

Ingrid said, “The photograph on your website is licensed to a trekking company in Quito.”

“The route is the same route whether I photographed it or not.”

“Fifty euros.”

Simón looked at her.

She held up the tobacco tin. “New agreement. You reach the ridge in my photograph and bring me back safely, you keep the fee. Every false fact costs fifty euros.”

Simón said, “You cannot change a contract by announcing it beside a mule.”

“You offered a refund you do not possess.”

Elías pulled the last strap tight. “I think the mule accepts her terms.”

Simón asked Ingrid why she needed him at all.

“I called five guides,” she said. “The honest ones told me the place did not exist.”

That was how they left: Ingrid walking ahead, Simón leading La Ministra, and Elías calling after them that the weather would reach the pass by three. A yellow dog followed for the first kilometer. Simón photographed it and sent the picture to his daughter. The dog turned back when the road became a trail.

Below the farms, the mountain was wet and green. The path entered a strip of cloud forest where moss covered the trunks and water moved under the stones. Ingrid used the walking stick on the high steps but refused Simón's hand. She kept a steady pace. Not a fast one, not a pace that would please the afternoon, but steady.

For the first hour she asked no questions about the shrine. She asked whether Simón was married, why La Ministra had one bent ear, how much his license cost, and whether the cracked red thermos tied to his pack was leaking onto her dress.

Simón said, “It does not leak.”

A brown drop struck the trail between them.

He added, “The thermos and I have an understanding.”

Ingrid watched another drop fall. Simón retied the thermos without comment.

She accepted coffee from it at the first stop. The cup tasted faintly of metal, but Ingrid drank all of it. She took one piece of ginger from the tobacco tin and bit it in half.

The name still troubled him. Santa Brígida del Nublado. He had typed it on a rainy Tuesday while trying to distinguish his pilgrimage from twelve other websites that offered sunrise, silence, and an encounter with the ancient Andes. Brígida had arrived from a phrase he remembered hearing as a child. The clouded one had been outside his window. He had added the beekeeper, the snowstorm, and a spring that cured indecision before dinner.

He had never found the name in a book.

Simón asked what her mother had said about the photograph.

Ingrid said, “That it was taken in June.”

“The back says June.”

“My mother was consistent.”

Simón asked, “Nothing else?”

Ingrid put the tin away. “Near the end, she said, ‘It was not a church when we needed it.’ Then she refused to say more.”

He asked what that meant.

“If I knew, I would have hired one of the honest men.”

The path climbed out of the trees. Low cloud moved across slopes of coarse grass and frailejones. Their pale leaves held beads of water. Behind them, the valley appeared and disappeared without getting farther away.

At noon they found the stone trough.

It stood exactly where Simón had placed it in the brochure: below a leaning cedar, at the bend where the trail crossed the old wall. The trough was long and narrow, cut from gray stone. Vines covered one end. Clear water spilled through a broken corner.

Simón stopped hard enough that La Ministra's nose struck his shoulder.

Ingrid looked from him to the trough. “You did not know.”

Simón said, “Of course I knew.”

Ingrid raised the tobacco tin. “One hundred euros.”

“You have never seen me surprised by a trough.”

“I have now.”

In the brochure it was the Pilgrims' Basin. Simón had written that travelers washed the dust from their hands before the final ascent. He had added a watercolor of three bent figures and given the basin a date two hundred years older than the country.

There were real grooves in the rim. He ran his thumb over one.

Ingrid asked, “Candles?”

“Ropes,” he said after a moment. “People tied animals here.”

She nodded. “Free of charge.”

He searched around the cedar. There was no cross, no foundation, no sign that anyone had prayed beside the trough. The old wall had once divided fields. A rusted horseshoe was pressed into the mud. Everything ordinary made the trough worse.

Ingrid filled her bottle. “Did you also invent the spring that cures indecision?”

“Yes.”

“Then it does not work.”

They continued.

An hour above the trough, the marked path curved east around a broad shoulder of the mountain. Ingrid stopped and took out the old photograph. She held it low, looking over its edge at the cloud.

Ingrid pointed toward the black rock. “We go left.”

Left was a narrow animal track rising toward black rock. Simón's route went right toward the abandoned maintenance shed where they were meant to sleep.

Simón said, “The trail is this way.”

“Your trail is based on a place that does not exist.”

“The mountain exists. We are trying to sleep on it.”

She left the marked path.

Simón tied La Ministra to a fence post and caught Ingrid after thirty meters. The animal track crossed a steep slope, but the ground beneath it was firm. It led toward a saddle with two stone points. One was square. The other leaned away as if broken at the root.

Ingrid raised the photograph. Her hand was trembling now, though it had not trembled on the climb.

Ingrid said, “My mother called it the broken tooth. She said the afternoon sun entered through the gap.”

The sun was nowhere. Cloud pressed across the saddle. Still, the angle of the two stones matched the faint shape behind the white building in the photograph.

Simón took the picture from her. There was the same notch, or one much like it. A mountain offered endless chances to recognize a thing you wanted.

From below the cloud came the sound of bells.

Not one bell. Many, striking without a common rhythm. The sound moved under them where no animals could be seen.

Ingrid closed her eyes.

Ingrid said, “She remembered bells.”

Simón listened. Some were sharp, some flat. One gave a double note because the clapper was loose.

He led her twenty meters down the slope. The cloud thinned. Brown cattle appeared below them, scattered through the grass. Each wore a bell on a wooden collar. A boy in a green poncho was sitting on a rock, looking at his phone.

Ingrid watched the animals for a long moment.

Ingrid said, “I was hoping for something less agricultural.”

“You wanted an explanation.”

“No. I wanted the privilege of rejecting one.”

The first drops of rain struck the photograph. Simón put it inside his coat before she could object. They returned to La Ministra and took the main path toward the shed.

They reached the maintenance shed at three-fifteen. The rain arrived with them.

The shed had been built for a cable line that no longer crossed the ridge. Three of its concrete walls were sound. The fourth was sheet metal. Wind pushed rain against the metal in loud handfuls. Inside were a wooden platform, two broken insulators, and a family of swallows that objected to the visitors from the rafters.

Simón unloaded La Ministra and brought her under the low roof at the back. He checked Ingrid's socks, made her change the wet pair, and set water to boil. She followed each instruction while making it clear that compliance was not surrender.

His daughter called while he was trying to make the thermos stand upright on the floor.

Lucía's face filled the screen. Behind her was the kitchen in her mother's apartment and a poster board covered with blue stars.

Lucía asked, “Did you find it?”

Simón said, “We had weather.”

“But did you find the shrine?”

Simón turned away from Ingrid. “Tomorrow. We have the German historian with us.”

Lucía asked to see her.

“She's working.”

Ingrid was sitting two meters away, peeling the wet label from a soup packet.

Lucía said, “You promised me a picture for the presentation.”

“Tomorrow, mi amor.”

“A real one. Not from the internet.”

Simón looked at the sheet-metal wall.

“A real one,” he said.

After the call, Ingrid poured soup into the two plastic cups. Then she asked, “German historian?”

“It is quicker than explaining.”

“To a child?”

“Especially to a child.”

The swallows shifted above them. Rain ran under the door and found the lowest part of the floor.

Ingrid said, “You make yourself more important for her.”

Simón said, “I make the work more interesting.”

“The work is interesting. You are on a mountain with an old woman who has discovered your imaginary saint.”

“That is not the version a father sends home.”

Ingrid ate her soup. “Perhaps it should be.”

Simón waited for the question about his daughter's mother, or the school account, or why a licensed guide had invented a saint. Ingrid asked none of them. Instead, she took the photograph from him and laid it between their cups.

Ingrid said, “I knew your brochure was false before I wrote to you.”

Simón asked how.

“The beekeeper. The snow. The footnote to a book that has no page seventy-three.”

He asked, “You ordered that book?”

“I wrote to its author. She was delighted. Nobody had contacted her about it in twelve years.”

Simón set down his cup. “You hired me because I am a criminal.”

“Your crimes are small and typographical. I hired you because of the ridge.”

She tapped the green line printed on his brochure. He had drawn the route from a survey map and satellite images. It ended at a viewpoint from which three ridges overlapped.

“Every other guide chose the valley with the chapel ruins,” Ingrid said. “Your invented route was the only one that approached the broken tooth from the angle in the photograph.”

Simón said, “Coincidence.”

“Certainly. We have had several.”

He asked why she needed a criminal.

“I needed someone who would not put my mother's story on a website.”

Outside, La Ministra stamped once under the lean-to. The rain softened, then came harder.

Ingrid opened the tobacco tin. Under the ginger was a folded piece of thin paper and a small red button with four holes. She did not remove either one.

“I was six,” she said. “I am the girl in the light coat. There was a dispute in the valley. Land, money, politics. The subject changed depending on who told it. Men came for two families after dark. Two women moved us up here before the men arrived.”

Simón said, “To the church.”

“My mother said it was not a church then. Perhaps a storehouse. Perhaps a clinic. We stayed three nights. Six children.”

Simón looked at the photograph. Six children stood before the white wall. One of the women held something long and dark against her skirt. He had assumed it was a walking stick.

He asked what had happened to the families.

“Some left. Some prospered. Some now tell the story differently.”

Simón asked for the names.

“No.”

He said, “You ask me to take you off a safe trail, but you won't tell me who was involved.”

“The dangerous ones are dead.”

He asked why she would not say.

“Their children have lawyers.”

He laughed. She did not.

“My mother took me to Germany two years later,” Ingrid said. “She made a complete life there. A good one. But every time I asked about Venezuela, she gave me a recipe or the name of a bird. I thought I remembered the bells until I understood that I remembered her saying the word. Near the end, she put this tin in my hand. I thought age would make her less secretive. It made her faster.”

Simón poured the last of the hot water into the cracked thermos. “And you are leaving the button?”

Ingrid closed the tin. “I have not decided.”

Simón said, “We may not reach the place.”

“You put a trough where a trough was. You put your path beneath the correct ridge. You remembered a name in my mother's handwriting.”

“I remembered weather.”

“You remembered something.”

He disliked the relief in her voice. He disliked more that he shared it.

Before they slept, he called Elías. The stream crossing below the saddle had risen. Elías said the rain had taken a piece of the upper path and would take more by morning.

“Turn back,” Elías said. “Bring me my mule and whatever remains of your business.”

Simón looked at Ingrid, already lying inside her sleeping bag with the tobacco tin against her chest.

“We turn back,” he said.

Ingrid closed her eyes.

By dawn the rain had stopped. Mist lay flat against the slope. Simón walked out alone and found the slide two hundred meters above the shed.

The animal track still ran toward the broken tooth, but a section of wet earth had fallen away. The gap was not wide. It was worse than wide. It was slanted, soft, and open above a gully full of rock. A careful person might cross it. A careful person could also pull the rest of it down.

When he returned, Ingrid had packed her sleeping bag.

Simón said, “We go down.”

Ingrid asked how far away the foundation was.

“There is no foundation.”

“How far is the place where there may be no foundation?”

“An hour, perhaps. It doesn't matter.”

“It matters to me.”

Simón said, “The slope moved.”

“Slopes move.”

“You hired the liar. You still have to obey the guide.”

She looked past him toward the mist. “Yesterday the guide followed me.”

“Yesterday you were right.”

“You do not know that I am wrong today.”

“Today the mountain is making the decision.”

Simón went behind the shed to saddle La Ministra. When he came back, Ingrid and her walking stick were gone.

He found her at the slide. She was three steps onto the wet section, moving sideways with her face toward the bank.

Simón called down, “Do not move.”

She moved.

Her uphill boot held. The earth beneath the other one folded. Ingrid dropped to one knee, struck the bank with her shoulder, and slid out of sight.

Simón heard her walking stick hit rock below. Then he heard nothing.

He lay flat at the edge. Ingrid had stopped eight meters down against a clump of woody grass. One leg was twisted under her. The tobacco tin was farther below, caught between two roots.

He said, “Look at me.”

She looked up.

He asked whether she was bleeding.

“I have not inspected everything.”

He asked about her neck, then her back. Neither hurt.

“Good. Stop trying to stand,” he said.

“I am not trying.”

“Your left hand is trying.”

She let go of the grass and put her hand flat against the dirt.

Simón moved without hurry because hurry traveled through a rope. He brought La Ministra to firm ground, tied her short, and took the climbing line from the pack. There was no tree large enough for an anchor. He looped webbing around a block of exposed stone, tested it in three directions, then added the mule's picket line to a second rock. He put on his harness and backed to the edge.

Ingrid watched all of it.

“You have done this before,” she said.

“That information is true.”

He lowered himself to her. The grass holding her had shallow roots. He clipped a loop around her chest before he touched her leg. Nothing looked broken. Her ankle had begun to swell, and a strip of skin was gone from her palm.

Simón said, “On three, you sit toward me.”

Ingrid asked about the tin.

“You first.”

“There are things in it—”

“Then you should have listened to the guide carrying the rope.”

She turned her face away. He thought she might argue. Instead she said, “Three.”

He helped her into the harness. She climbed the rope with her good leg while he kept her away from the loose bank. At the top, she rolled onto firm grass and stayed there, breathing through her nose.

Simón went down again.

The tin was lodged within reach of the line. He freed it from the roots and put it inside his jacket. By the time he climbed back, Ingrid was sitting up.

He handed her the tin.

Ingrid said, “You should not have done that.”

“Correct.”

“Thank you.”

“Eat the ginger.”

He wrapped her ankle. She ate two pieces without objecting. After ten minutes she could put weight on the foot without sharp pain. The swelling was small.

Until then, every step toward the ridge had been Ingrid's bad decision. Simón could still take her back to the shed and begin the long descent. Instead, he tested the higher ground beyond the slide. He found a route around it through exposed rock. The detour was steep but stable, the weather was clearing, and it would add forty minutes. He wanted to see what was there.

When he returned, Ingrid had opened her phone to the payment screen.

Simón asked, “What are you doing?”

“Paying the second half.”

“We have not reached anything.”

“The rescue is not free.”

“It is included.”

“In the fraudulent pilgrimage?”

He took the phone from her and locked it. “You can pay me when we are down.”

He expected her to insist on turning back now that the decision was hers. Ingrid put the phone away and asked whether the route around the slide would hold.

“Yes,” he said.

Ingrid asked, “Do you know, or are you being brave for your daughter?”

“I know.”

They left La Ministra at the shed. Ingrid's ankle would take weight, but Simón tied a short safety line between them for the steep ground. He carried both packs. They crossed above the slide and climbed into the saddle.

The cloud opened without drama.

There was no chapel on the ridge. There was a rectangle in the grass.

Four low runs of stone marked a building about the size of Elías's shed. Young trees grew inside it. At one corner, a rusted drainpipe pointed downhill. No cross stood there. No plaque. No saint. The only color came from small yellow flowers growing between the stones.

Ingrid stopped at the edge of the rectangle.

Ingrid held out her hand. “Photograph.”

Simón gave her the old one. She walked twenty paces downhill, limping, then five to the west. She held the picture up.

From that exact place, the broken tooth rose behind the foundation. A notch in the lower ridge matched the edge of the white roof. One of the young trees obscured the view, but not enough.

The building had been here.

Whether it had been a church was another question. The stones held no answer. Simón found a bit of white plaster under the moss and half a ceramic drain tile. He found a bottle cap that could not have been older than ten years. The foundation might have lasted a century or been rebuilt last summer.

Ingrid entered the rectangle. She touched one of the trees, then sat on a stone and opened the tobacco tin.

She took out the red button. Its surface was scratched and dull. After that came the folded paper.

“Six names,” she said. “Only first names. My mother wrote them from memory.”

Simón moved away while she unfolded it. The paper made almost no sound in the wind.

Ingrid used the end of her walking stick to loosen the earth inside the foundation. Simón gave her his small trowel. She dug a narrow hole, folded the list twice, and pressed it into the ground.

The red button remained in her palm.

Simón asked, “That too?”

“No.”

She filled the hole and placed one flat stone over it. Then she put the button back in the tin.

“I thought I came to leave it,” she said.

“You left something.”

“Do not become philosophical. I will subtract another fifty.”

He took her photograph from the same place where the old photographer had stood. Ingrid did not smile. She held the tobacco tin in one hand and the walking stick in the other. Behind her, the rectangle looked like what it was: stones, trees, a broken pipe, and enough white plaster to support an argument.

While she rested, Simón compared the two photographs.

The six children were clearer in the old picture now that he knew where to look. A small girl in front wore a light coat. One button near the collar was missing. Behind her stood the two women.

The woman at the edge was not holding a walking stick.

It was an ice axe. The dark shaft ran from her hand to the ground. Just below her fist was a pale metal band, folded at an angle. Two black rivets sat in it, one higher than the other.

Simón enlarged the photograph on his phone until the image broke into squares. The repair remained.

Copper around a split wooden shaft. Two crooked rivets.

His grandfather's axe was hanging above Elías's workbench.

Ingrid asked, “What is it?”

Simón looked from the photograph to the empty ridge.

His grandfather had guided surveyors, priests, smugglers, and anyone else who could pay. He had died without sorting his stories into true and false. An axe could be lent. It could be bought, stolen, traded, or left behind. There were a hundred ways for it to reach the woman's hand.

There were fewer ways for the same name to reach two pieces of writing seventy years apart.

“Nothing,” Simón said.

Ingrid held out her hand. “That one was free. We are no longer climbing.”

He returned the photograph.

They reached the shed after noon. La Ministra greeted them by trying to eat Simón's rain cover. Ingrid rode the mule for the steepest hour and walked the rest. Below the trough, the clouds lifted and the valley came back around them.

Ingrid kept her account aloud.

“One hundred euros deducted for two lies,” she said. “One hundred added for the rescue.”

Simón said, “The rescue was included.”

“Then I add it as a gratuity.”

“You cannot deduct and return the same money. That is theatrical accounting.”

“Yes.”

He asked, “And the other seven hundred?”

“Payable when the mule delivers me.”

La Ministra stopped to urinate in the center of the trail.

“The mule is reconsidering the contract,” Simón said.

At the roadhead, two hikers were reading the wooden sign beside the gate. Simón had painted it himself in his office and paid a cousin to drive it up. A white arrow pointed toward the mountain. Under it, in blue letters, were the words SANTA BRÍGIDA DEL NUBLADO, PILGRIM ROUTE.

The hikers had clean boots and small day packs. One was photographing the arrow.

One of them asked, “Is the shrine this way?”

Simón put down Ingrid's pack.

Before he could answer, Ingrid said, “The upper path is closed after the rain. The mountain has made the decision.”

The hikers looked at her muddy trousers, the bandage around her ankle, and the old walking stick. They thanked her and went back to their car.

Simón took a wrench from La Ministra's pack. The sign was fixed to the gate with two bolts. One had rusted. Ingrid braced the board while he worked it loose.

“Your daughter still needs a photograph,” she said.

“I have one.”

“A real one?”

“Real enough to cause trouble.”

The last bolt came free. The false sign dropped into their hands.

They carried it down between them.
